Hiring

I like to start with something a candidate knows well and ask progressively deeper questions. A few follow-ups often tell me a lot about their curiosity, how thoroughly they understand their work, and what interests them enough to keep digging.

I'm skeptical of LeetCode-style questions as a way to identify good engineers. I prefer logic problems that let me see how someone structures their thinking. Can they follow an argument through to its conclusion? What happens when I offer a counterexample?

Those abilities matter with AI tools, too. I want engineers who can follow a line of reasoning to the end and consider counterexamples, even when using AI tools.

Product strategy

I try to understand where a product can create additional value given the role it already plays for customers.

For a smaller recruiting platform, becoming an applicant tracking system can sound attractive: deeper integration, more switching costs. But if customers use the platform as a supplemental recruiting source, asking it to become their system of record may misunderstand why they buy it.

I ask a similar question about selling a candidate database. Customer demand is worth taking seriously, but I want to examine what else we could produce from the same information. Could our existing signals support useful forecasts or advice?

I see more value in turning those signals into useful forecasts or advice than in reselling the underlying data.

Advertising

I think of advertising as allocating scarce human attention. Advertisers face a tradeoff between urgency and efficiency, and we have a finite number of impressions from which to generate meaningful conversions.

Improving engagement and application rates can make that attention more productive. But removing every step can also make it easier to submit applications with very little intent. A higher conversion count can hide a worse outcome for the employer.

Some friction is useful when it helps establish that a person has considered the opportunity. I care about whether an application represents real interest and whether the employer gets something valuable from it. I think optimizing for low-intent volume pushes the industry toward commoditization and a race to the bottom.

Learning and doing

Jim Keller influenced how I think about engineering, especially the balance between learning and doing. My instinct has usually been to slow down and understand a problem thoroughly. Years of engineering have taught me to ask whether more understanding will change the decision in front of me.

Sometimes it will, and taking the time is worthwhile. Other times, the better choice is to act on what we know and move to the next priority. I think I have a better balance now. I still value understanding, but I’m more deliberate about where it helps and when it’s time to get something done.
