World action models · KV caches · an explorable

From 300 cache reads to 10

What happens inside Fast-WAM, a world action model for robot arms, each time it picks its next 32 moves. And the one structural change that lets Faster-WAM do the same job with one thirtieth of the action-layer passes.

The short answer

Fast-WAM runs a big video transformer once on the camera image and keeps every layer's keys and values: its KV cache. Then a 30-layer action expert turns random noise into 32 actions over 10 denoising steps. Each action layer reads the cache of its matching video layer, so one chunk costs 30 × 10 = 300 reads.

Faster-WAM learns one weighted mix of all 30 caches, builds it once per chunk, and keeps a single action layer. That layer reads the fused cache at each step: 1 × 10 = 10 reads.

The big picture

01One action chunk, two models, one clock

A robot arm on the LIBERO benchmark looks at one camera frame, reads an instruction such as “put the bowl on the plate”, and outputs an action chunk: its next 32 moves, 7 numbers each. It does this again every time it replans, so the time per chunk decides how quickly the robot can react.

Both models make the chunk the same way. The video model, the 5-billion-parameter Wan2.2 transformer, runs its 30 layers once on the image and leaves each layer's keys (K) and values (V) in a cache. Then the action expert starts from pure noise and cleans it up in 10 flow-matching steps. At each step it reads the cache and predicts a direction, and the chunk moves a little toward a clean motion. Most of the noise only goes away in the last three steps, because the schedule spends its early steps at high noise. Third, Faster-WAM's whole denoising loop is shorter than one of Fast-WAM's steps on the A100 bf16 clock. Use Step → to walk through its 10 steps one at a time.

Step 1 · inputs

02What goes in: tokens

A transformer works on tokens: one vector of numbers per piece of input. Fast-WAM has four kinds.

  • Video. Two 224 × 224 camera views (agent and wrist) sit side by side as one 224 × 448 image. The Wan2.2 VAE compresses it 16 times in each direction, and the transformer groups 2 × 2 latent cells into one token. That leaves 7 × 14 = 98 video tokens, each 3,072 numbers wide.
  • Text. The UMT5-XXL text encoder turns the instruction into up to 128 tokens.
  • Robot state. 8 numbers that describe the robot's current state are projected to 1 extra token that joins the text tokens.
  • Actions. The chunk is 32 time steps × 7 numbers. It starts as pure noise. Each time step is one action token, 1,024 numbers wide inside the action expert.
patchify(camera) → 98 tokens videotext + stateaction
scene is a drawn stand-in for a LIBERO frame

Fast-WAM's own idea, from its paper's title “Do world action models need test-time future imagination?”, is that the video model does not have to imagine future frames when the robot acts. It encodes only the frame it can see. That is why one video pass per chunk is enough.

The change

03Lite KV-Fusion: one cache made from thirty

Here's the one piece of attention you need for the rest of this page. Each video layer turns every image token into a key and a value. When an action token reads the image, it compares its query with all 98 keys, and the better a key matches, the more of that token's value it takes in. The video tokens never look at the actions, so their keys and values stay the same through all 10 denoising steps. Fast-WAM computes them once per chunk and stores them. That store is the KV cache, one set of keys and values for each of the 30 video layers.

Fast-WAM pairs the layers. Action layer j reads cache j and nothing else, so 30 action layers times 10 steps makes 300 reads per chunk. The pairing is also why you can't just delete action layers: drop action layer 17 and nothing reads video layer 17 anymore.

Faster-WAM breaks the pairing. It mixes the 30 caches into one fused cache, once per chunk, and a single action layer reads that at every step. For each attention head it learns one number per video layer, turns those 30 numbers into weights that add up to 1, and takes the weighted sum:

Here ℓ is the video layer, 1 to 30, and h is the attention head, 1 to 24. The αhℓ are the learned numbers. The softmax turns them into weights βhℓ that are positive and add up to 1. Kℓh and Vℓh are layer ℓ's cached keys and values for head h, and the hats mark the fused cache. Keys and values share the same weights.

That's 24 × 30 = 720 learned numbers for the whole fusion. They start at zero, so training starts from a plain average with every β = 1/30.

What does training learn? In the paper's Figure 4 (LIBERO), every learned weight stays between 0.030 and 0.037, close to 1/30 ≈ 0.033 (read off the figure by eye). The middle of the stack, about layers 12 to 19, gets a little more, and the pattern changes from head to head. So the fused cache is close to an average of the whole video stack. It still beats reading only the last video layer: in the paper's step-by-step ablation (Figure 3), fusion goes from 97.7% to 98.0% on LIBERO and from 60.3% to 68.7% on LIBERO-Plus, where the same scenes are perturbed. Paper

Fusion also makes the depth a free choice. Any number of action layers can read all 30 video layers now, so the action expert doesn't have to be 30 deep. The paper only tests one layer, and it says that doesn't make one layer the best depth.

Two smaller changes

04Changes that travel with fusion

World-model-only conditioning

In Fast-WAM, every action layer also cross-attends to the 128 text tokens and the robot-state token, at every step: another 300 reads per chunk. Faster-WAM deletes that cross-attention. The instruction and robot state still reach the actions, but only through the video transformer, which reads them in every layer and writes what it learned into its cache. The paper's argument is that this keeps the small action expert from leaning on the text and state directly and helps the policy generalise.

Retracted 1D RoPE: put the keys on the action's clock

Attention also needs to know where tokens are. Both experts use rotary position encoding (RoPE): each query and key is cut into 64 pairs of numbers, and each pair is turned by an angle that grows with the token's position. When a query meets a key, the score in each pair depends only on the difference between their two angles. That difference is what tells the layer how far apart two tokens are, and it only means something if both sides turn by the same coordinate at the same rate.

Here they don't. The video model turns 22 pairs of every key by frame time, 21 by row and 21 by column. The action expert turns all 64 pairs of every query by the action step. Let's follow one example through the rest of this section: action step 5 reads the patch at row 3, column 4 of the camera frame, and that frame sits at time 0.

In the 22 time pairs things already work. The frame is at time 0, a turn by 0 is no turn, and the score sees a clean gap of 5 steps. The two sides do turn their time pairs at different rates, which would matter for a frame at any other time, but Faster-WAM's actions only read time 0.

The other 42 pairs subtract 5 steps from 3 rows, or from 4 columns. Take the first row pair. The key turns 1 radian per row and the query 0.042 radians per step, so the score sees 3 − 0.042 × 5 = 2.79. At step 6 the same patch gives 2.75, as if it had moved 0.04 rows. Nothing in the image moved. Other pairs drift at other rates, from 0.002 to about 15 rows or columns per step, so they don't even agree with each other. Derived So each action step reads the image with its own skewed sense of where things are, and that skew comes from token order, not from the scene. The paper proves this as its Proposition 2.3.

Faster-WAM's fix puts the keys on the action expert's clock. After it builds the fused cache, it turns each fused key back by its own video angles, which removes the time, row and column turn. Then it turns the key forward by the action expert's angle for the frame's time. The frame is at time 0, so that second turn does nothing, and in practice the whole fix is the undo. The query keeps its turn for step 5. Now all 64 pairs see the same thing, that the action is 5 steps after the frame, at the action expert's own rates. That's the same way two action tokens already compare with each other. The key's content can still carry what the video model worked out about where things are, because the video model keeps its 3D rotation inside its own 30 layers. Only the explicit turn on the keys handed to the action expert is gone.

In symbols, here's the fix, then the score between action step i and the video token at position p, before and after:

  • i is the action step, 0 to 31. In the example, i = 5.
  • p = (t, y, x) is the video token's frame time, row and column. In the example, p = (0, 3, 4).
  • j runs over the 64 pairs of one head. qj is the query's pair before any turn, and k̄j(p) is the fused key's pair with no turn.
  • k̂(p) is the fused key as it comes out of the cache, turned by the video angles.
  • R(θ) turns a pair by the angle θ. Rv(p) applies the video turns to all 64 pairs, and Ra(t) applies the action expert's turns for time t. The transpose ⊤ turns a rotation back, and Ra(0) turns nothing.
  • νj is the action expert's rate for pair j, in radians per step. ωj is the video model's rate for pair j, in radians per frame, row or column, and b(j) says which of t, y or x that pair uses.

With the example, the first row pair has ω = 1 and ν = 0.042. Before the fix its angle is 1 × 3 − 0.042 × 5 = 2.79. After the fix it's 0.042 × (0 − 5) = −0.21, the same for every patch in the frame.

Which comes first, the fusion or the undo? The fusion. The cache holds keys that are already turned, and all 30 layers turn the token at position p by exactly the same angles. A weighted sum of keys that were all turned the same way equals that turn applied to the weighted sum. So undoing it after the fusion gives exactly the key you'd get by undoing it in every layer first (the paper's Proposition 2.2), with one undo per token instead of thirty. The LeRobot code works in that order: fuse the cached keys, then undo the turn on the fused keys, once per chunk. Values never get turned, so they're left alone.

How much does it matter? Less than the math might suggest. In the paper's step-by-step ablation (Figure 3), adding retracted RoPE to the model without text cross-attention moves LIBERO from 97.8% to 98.3% and LIBERO-Plus from 77.6% to 78.3%. Paper The paper's other fix, giving action tokens stand-in spatial positions (lifted 3D RoPE), reached 98.2% and 74.0%. The authors guess the mismatch matters more once the robot state can only reach the actions through the video model. The figure doesn't say how many runs each intermediate row averages.

Measured

05Where the time goes

Fewer passes only help if the passes were the slow part. They were. Pick a source: the paper's own timing, or the LeRobot implementation timed on an A100.

latency(one chunk)

The action loop shrinks by a factor of 24 to 34, depending on the source. The whole chunk shrinks much less, because the image encoder, the text encoder and the video prefill stay the same. In Faster-WAM those fixed parts are now most of the time, so the next speedup has to come from them.

Why bf16 shows 5.3× and fp32 shows 2.5× eager PyTorch overhead

In the LeRobot runs, the default model is slower in bf16 (938.0 ms) than in fp32 (802.2 ms). Measured The traces point to launch overhead rather than arithmetic:

  • The default bf16 denoiser launches 29,020 GPU kernels per chunk; Faster-WAM's launches 770. That is about 97 kernels per action-layer pass versus 77 Derived.
  • The default bf16 denoiser performs 8,140 dtype conversions per chunk, versus 640 in fp32.
  • Replaying the unchanged default bf16 denoiser through a CUDA graph, with bit-identical outputs, cut the default policy from 868.2 ms to 283.5 ms.

When each pass costs mostly the work of launching small kernels, cutting 300 passes to 10 removes almost all of that cost. So the bf16 ratio mostly measures eager-mode overhead, not a ratio of arithmetic. Compiled or graph-captured comparisons of both models were not measured.

Read this before you quote a number

07Limits and sources

Limits

  • The LeRobot timings use random video and action weights and real pretrained VAE and text encoders. They measure speed, not task quality. No trained Faster-WAM policy was run.
  • The paper timed a 24 GiB consumer GPU with parts of Fast-WAM measured separately; LeRobot timed an A100 end to end including tokenization and transfers. Do not compare the two sets of milliseconds directly.
  • The LeRobot ratios moved between sessions (fp32 2.66× → 2.51×, bf16 5.10× → 5.34×). The intervals cover sampling within one session only.
  • The pictures simplify. The action curves in the race are illustrative, and the race shades its fusion lines with a head-average profile read off the paper's Figure 4 by eye. The two before/after figures in sections 03 and 04 are schematics, not measured weights or attention scores. Token counts, layer counts, widths, the step schedule and all read counts match the model and code.
  • The RoPE example uses the rates in the LeRobot code (base 10,000, 64 action pairs, video pairs split 22/21/21). The drift range of 0.002 to about 15 rows or columns per step is derived from the paper's Eq. 9 with those rates.
  • “Read” here means one action-layer pass that attends to a cache. Each pass also runs projections and a feed-forward network; the saving comes from running 10 passes instead of 300, not from memory traffic alone.
