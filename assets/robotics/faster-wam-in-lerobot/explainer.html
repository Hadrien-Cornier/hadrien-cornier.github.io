World action models · KV caches · an explorable

From 300 cache reads to 10

What happens inside Fast-WAM, a world action model for robot arms, each time it picks its next 32 moves. And the one structural change that lets Faster-WAM do the same job with one thirtieth of the action-layer passes.

The short answer

Fast-WAM runs a big video transformer once on the camera image and keeps every layer's keys and values: its KV cache. Then a 30-layer action expert turns random noise into 32 actions over 10 denoising steps. Each action layer reads the cache of its matching video layer, so one chunk costs 30 × 10 = 300 reads.

Faster-WAM learns one weighted mix of all 30 caches, builds it once per chunk, and keeps a single action layer. That layer reads the fused cache at each step: 1 × 10 = 10 reads.

The big picture

01One action chunk, two models, one clock

A robot arm on the LIBERO benchmark looks at one camera frame, reads an instruction such as “put the bowl on the plate”, and outputs an action chunk: its next 32 moves, 7 numbers each. It does this again every time it replans, so the time per chunk decides how quickly the robot can react.

Both models make the chunk the same way. The video model, the 5-billion-parameter Wan2.2 transformer, runs its 30 layers once on the image and leaves each layer's keys (K) and values (V) in a cache. Then the action expert starts from pure noise and cleans it up in 10 flow-matching steps. At each step it reads the cache and predicts a direction, and the chunk moves a little toward a clean motion. Three things to notice. First, the video model does the same work in both: 30 layers, once per chunk. Second, the chunk goes through the same 10 noise levels in both models. Most of the noise only goes away in the last three steps, because the schedule spends its early steps at high noise. Third, Faster-WAM's whole denoising loop is shorter than one of Fast-WAM's steps on the A100 bf16 clock. Use Step → to walk through its 10 steps one at a time.

Step 1 · inputs

02What goes in: tokens

A transformer works on tokens: one vector of numbers per piece of input. Fast-WAM has four kinds.

  • Video. Two 224 × 224 camera views (agent and wrist) sit side by side as one 224 × 448 image. The Wan2.2 VAE compresses it 16 times in each direction, and the transformer groups 2 × 2 latent cells into one token. That leaves 7 × 14 = 98 video tokens, each 3,072 numbers wide.
  • Text. The UMT5-XXL text encoder turns the instruction into up to 128 tokens.
  • Robot state. 8 numbers that describe the robot's current state are projected to 1 extra token that joins the text tokens.
  • Actions. The chunk is 32 time steps × 7 numbers. It starts as pure noise. Each time step is one action token, 1,024 numbers wide inside the action expert.
patchify(camera) → 98 tokens videotext + stateaction
scene is a drawn stand-in for a LIBERO frame

Fast-WAM's own idea, from its paper's title “Do world action models need test-time future imagination?”, is that the video model does not have to imagine future frames when the robot acts. It encodes only the frame it can see. That is why one video pass per chunk is enough.

Step 2 · attention

03How one token reads the others

Inside every layer, each token asks a question and every token offers an answer. The question is the query vector q. The label on each answer is the key vector k. The content behind it is the value vector v. A layer makes all three by multiplying each token's vector by three learned matrices.

How well a query matches a key is their dot product. Divide by √d, add a mask, and apply softmax along the row: you get weights that sum to 1. The token's output is the weighted sum of the values.

The mask M decides who may read whom. In Fast-WAM, video tokens read only video tokens. Action tokens read the observed frame's video tokens and all the action tokens. So the video side never depends on the actions. Keep that in mind: it is what makes the cache possible.

Step 3 · caching

04The KV cache: do the video work once

The action chunk changes at every denoising step, so the action tokens' queries, keys and values must be computed again at every step. The video tokens do not change. The camera frame is clean, and the mask stops video tokens from reading actions. So a video layer's keys and values are the same at step 1 and at step 10.

Fast-WAM therefore runs the video tower once per chunk, a prefill, and keeps each layer's K and V. That store is the KV cache. One layer's cache holds 98 tokens × 3,072 numbers for K and the same for V: 602,112 numbers. There are 30 of them.

Then, at each denoising step, action layer j reads cache j. The ledger counts the work for one chunk. Compare it with the counterfactual where nothing is cached.

    The change

    06Lite KV-Fusion: one cache made from thirty

    Fast-WAM pairs its layers: action layer j can read only video layer j. With that pairing, removing action layers also removes access to the video layers they were paired with. Faster-WAM breaks the pairing. For each attention head h it learns 30 numbers α, one per video layer, turns them into weights with a softmax, and mixes the 30 cached keys and values:

    Keys and values share the same weights. With 24 heads, 30 video layers and 1 action layer, that is 24 × 30 × 1 = 720 learned numbers: the whole fusion module. The logits start at zero, so training starts from a plain average, every β = 1/30. The mix is computed once per chunk and reused for all 10 steps.

    What did training learn? In the paper's Figure 4 (LIBERO), every learned weight stays between 0.030 and 0.037, close to 1/30 ≈ 0.033. So the fused cache is close to an average of the whole video hierarchy, tilted toward the middle. The paper compares this against reading the final layer only and finds fusion better both on LIBERO and under distribution shift.

    Two smaller changes

    07Changes that travel with fusion

    World-model-only conditioning

    In Fast-WAM, every action layer also cross-attends to the 128 text tokens and the robot-state token, at every step: another 300 reads per chunk. Faster-WAM deletes that cross-attention. The instruction and robot state still reach the actions, but only through the video transformer, which reads them in every layer and writes what it learned into its cache. The paper's argument is that this keeps the small action expert from leaning on the text and state directly and helps the policy generalise.

    Retracted 1D-RoPE

    Transformers mark position by rotating pairs of numbers inside each query and key (rotary position encoding, RoPE). The video transformer rotates by time, row and column; the action expert rotates by the action's time index only. When an action query meets a video key with both native rotations, the angles do not line up. Faster-WAM undoes the video rotation of the fused key and re-applies the action's rotation for time 0.

    Measured

    08Where the time goes

    Fewer passes only help if the passes were the slow part. They were. Pick a source: the paper's own timing, or the LeRobot implementation timed on an A100.

    latency(one chunk)

    The action loop shrinks by a factor of 24 to 34, depending on the source. The whole chunk shrinks much less, because the image encoder, the text encoder and the video prefill stay the same. In Faster-WAM those fixed parts are now most of the time, so the next speedup has to come from them.

    Why bf16 shows 5.3× and fp32 shows 2.5× eager PyTorch overhead

    In the LeRobot runs, the default model is slower in bf16 (938.0 ms) than in fp32 (802.2 ms). Measured The traces point to launch overhead rather than arithmetic:

    • The default bf16 denoiser launches 29,020 GPU kernels per chunk; Faster-WAM's launches 770. That is about 97 kernels per action-layer pass versus 77 Derived.
    • The default bf16 denoiser performs 8,140 dtype conversions per chunk, versus 640 in fp32.
    • Replaying the unchanged default bf16 denoiser through a CUDA graph, with bit-identical outputs, cut the default policy from 868.2 ms to 283.5 ms.

    When each pass costs mostly the work of launching small kernels, cutting 300 passes to 10 removes almost all of that cost. So the bf16 ratio mostly measures eager-mode overhead, not a ratio of arithmetic. Compiled or graph-captured comparisons of both models were not measured.

    Read this before you quote a number

    10Limits and sources

    Limits

    • The LeRobot timings use random video and action weights and real pretrained VAE and text encoders. They measure speed, not task quality. No trained Faster-WAM policy was run.
    • The paper timed a 24 GiB consumer GPU with parts of Fast-WAM measured separately; LeRobot timed an A100 end to end including tokenization and transfers. Do not compare the two sets of milliseconds directly.
    • The LeRobot ratios moved between sessions (fp32 2.66× → 2.51×, bf16 5.10× → 5.34×). The intervals cover sampling within one session only.
    • The animations simplify. The attention widget uses toy numbers. Cache tiles, scores and action curves are illustrative pictures; token counts, layer counts, widths, the step schedule and all read counts match the model and code. The paper-shaped fusion profile was read off Figure 4 by eye.
    • “Read” here means one action-layer pass that attends to a cache. Each pass also runs projections and a feed-forward network; the saving comes from running 10 passes instead of 300, not from memory traffic alone.
