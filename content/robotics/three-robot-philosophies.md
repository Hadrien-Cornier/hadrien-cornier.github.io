you want a robot that does the chores in your home. It folds the towels, picks up the toys and makes the bed, about 50 small jobs a day. The natural way to teach it is to show it how people do these things, and there is a nearly endless supply of video of people doing exactly that. But a person has legs, two five-fingered hands and eyes at head height, and your robot might have wheels, two pincer grippers and a camera on its wrist.</p><p>So does a robot need a human body to learn from human demonstrations? The answer decides a lot about price, because legs and many-jointed hands are a lot of hardware next to an arm on a wheeled base. Three companies are betting on different answers. Figure builds humanoids and trains them first on human video. Generalist records people working with handheld grippers that match the robot's grippers. Physical Intelligence (Pi) collects experience from many kinds of robot and lets them practice. Keep the towel in mind: we need to learn what to do with it, then turn that knowledge into movements our robot can actually make. Let's take the chore robot through each bet.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='nines' data-view='nines' data-title='How many failures a month?'></div><h2 id='sec-nines'>How good is good enough?</h2><p>First, let's put a number on good enough. Our robot does 50 tasks a day, which is 1,500 tasks in a 30-day month. Say you'd put up with one failure a month on average (a towel in the sink, a toy left on the stairs). That's a per-task failure rate of 1/1,500, or about 99.93% success. A robot that succeeds 99% of the time sounds great, but in your house it fails 0.5 times a day, which is a mess roughly every 2 days. (The workload is my illustration. Plug in your own household.)</p><p>Dmitri Dolgov of Waymo has a <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Gp4zrV3-6N8'>rule of thumb from self-driving cars</a>: every extra nine, from 90% to 99% to 99.9% to 99.99%, costs about 10 times more effort. He also explained why the target changes the design. Plain end-to-end imitation of human drivers made fast early progress, but it isn't enough for a car that is far more reliable than a human. And a camera covered in dust has no information about a pedestrian that a lidar can still see. If human-level driving is the goal, cameras alone can be fine; the stronger goal needs more. Our chore robot works the same way: the product sets the reliability bar, and the bar decides the body, the sensors and the data.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='fork' data-view='fork' data-title='Coverage versus actions'></div><h2 id='sec-fork'>No data source gives you both coverage and actions</h2><p>To fold a towel, the robot needs two kinds of information. It needs coverage: what towels, bathrooms and laundry baskets look like across thousands of homes, and which step comes next. And it needs actions: where its own gripper goes at each instant and how hard it squeezes. Each source of data lands at a different spot on those two axes.</p><p>Human video has enormous coverage, but it has no record of actions or forces. You can watch the hand move; you can't see the torques. Even the hand position is fuzzy: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20290'>EgoZero</a> reports hand-tracking errors of 1 to 2 cm, a lot when you're pinching a towel corner. Robot logs are the opposite: exact actions and forces for that robot, but every hour is expensive, so they cover few homes. A handheld gripper sits in between. A person carries a camera-equipped gripper through real homes, recording actions that directly match the gripper the robot will use.</p><p>When you move from human video to a robot with a different body, some things carry over: the layout of the home, the objects, the order of the steps, navigation and the rough path of the hand. The exact grasp, the finger contacts and the camera view are hard to match on a wheeled robot with grippers, and whole-body motion is something a humanoid can use and a wheeled gripper can't. Each company chose a corner of this plot and built its hardware to fit. Figure made the body human so human video lines up with it. Generalist made the recording tool match the robot. Pi records the robots themselves.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='homes' data-view='homes' data-title='Figure: one change, human-video pretraining'></div><h2 id='sec-homes'>Figure makes the body match the video</h2><p>Figure's bet is that if the robot moves like you, video of you is nearly robot training data. So Figure builds the hands and the rest of the robot itself. It also builds the model, Helix, and collects the human video, Index. The idea is to make the robot's movements line up with the movements in those videos. Brett Adcock, the founder, claims the fingertips match human fingertips to millimeter precision; Figure hasn't published how it measured that.</p><p>The strongest evidence is a controlled comparison in the <a href='https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-2-5-zero-shot-30-home-generalization'>Helix 2.5 report</a>. Two copies of the same model learn from the same robot task data and face the same evaluation. One of them first learns from Index human video alone, starting from a blank model; the other skips that step. That first stage is what we mean by pretraining. Both were then tested in 30 Bay Area homes where nobody had collected data. Towel folding went from 12 successes in 140 tries to 87. Across towels, toys and beds, success went from 35/420 to 237/420, about 8% to 56%. That's a big jump from one change, and I'm convinced human video helps. It's also where Figure puts its weight: on broad pretraining, more than on training for each specific task afterward.</p><p>Look at what the test varies, though. The homes and objects were new, but the robot had trained on these tasks elsewhere, with robot data. And no wheeled robot or plain arm was in the comparison, so the test can't tell us whether the human body was necessary. Adcock argues that it is: in <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AoFwpf43wK0'>his talk</a> he says data recorded through tools is structurally limited, and a robot that moves like a human has a higher, maybe unbounded, ceiling. Those are predictions. Figure's own history shows a different kind of limit: moving from a table robot to a mobile one took about a year of Helix redesign to control every joint, a problem with the model's architecture rather than with the data.</p><figure class='rc-video'><div style='position:relative;width:100%;aspect-ratio:16/9'><iframe style='position:absolute;inset:0;width:100%;height:100%;border:0' src='https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/lJpM_2a1zrE' title='Figure robot in an unfamiliar home' loading='lazy' allow='accelerometer; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; fullscreen' referrerpolicy='strict-origin-when-cross-origin' allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption>Figure's demonstration in an unfamiliar home. <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lJpM_2a1zrE'>Watch on YouTube</a>.</figcaption></figure><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='device' data-view='device' data-title='Generalist: people hold the robot gripper'></div><h2 id='sec-device'>Generalist makes the tool match the robot</h2><p>Generalist goes the other way. Instead of building a human body, it puts the robot's hand in human hands. People do tasks holding camera-equipped grippers that match the grippers on the robot, so each recording already shows the robot's kind of action from the robot's kind of camera. Generalist had 270,000 hours of this by November 2025 and more than 500,000 by April 2026, and its <a href='https://generalistai.com/blog/gen-0'>GEN-0</a> and GEN-1 base models pretrain on it with no robot data at all.</p><p>On 3 trained tasks, with about 1 hour of robot data per task, <a href='https://generalistai.com/blog/gen-1'>GEN-1</a> averages 99% success, against 64% for GEN-0 and 19% for a model trained from scratch. Generalist didn't publish trial counts. Does the idea work outside Generalist? An academic group (<a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18647'>Lin et al.</a>) tried a similar handheld device on 4 tasks in 8 new places, with unfamiliar objects and no robot training data. Success ranged from 85% to 92.5%. That's encouraging evidence for the recording method, though it's a related device and not a test of Generalist's model.</p><p>The founders say hours alone won't do it. Generalist's <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-TTAOxVN2eo'>Andrew Barry</a> stresses that the collected data must match what the robot sees and does when deployed. <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Gy4GbCCSwI'>Pete Florence</a> says hours aren't interchangeable: you also need fast, natural collection, a model big enough to use it and quick tests of which <a href='https://generalistai.com/blog/physical-commonsense'>data mixtures</a> help. In August 2026 the company showed <a href='https://generalistai.com/blog/gen-1.5'>GEN-1.5</a> learning simple short tasks from one demonstration or a few minutes of data, without a quantitative evaluation. The open risk is new hands. Generalist <a href='https://generalistai.com/blog/towards-machines-with-a-thousand-hands'>bets</a> that the physics of handling objects carries over between hands, and Florence <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LpHxwtKRPdQ'>says</a> generalizing to a new hand is still unsolved.</p><figure class='rc-video'><div style='position:relative;width:100%;aspect-ratio:16/9'><iframe style='position:absolute;inset:0;width:100%;height:100%;border:0' src='https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/1cllCVK-9lo' title='Generalist GEN-1.5 short-task adaptation' loading='lazy' allow='accelerometer; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; fullscreen' referrerpolicy='strict-origin-when-cross-origin' allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption>Generalist's GEN-1.5 adapting to short tasks. <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1cllCVK-9lo'>Watch on YouTube</a>.</figcaption></figure><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='pi' data-view='pi' data-title='Pi: many robots, then practice'></div><h2 id='sec-pi'>Pi collects many robots and lets them practice</h2><p>Pi bets on variety. You give its model a camera image and an instruction such as "fold the towel," and it outputs robot movements. These are called vision-language-action models, or VLAs, and Pi trains them mostly on recordings from robots. Its own robots are mostly two-arm machines, fixed or on wheels, all with parallel-jaw grippers, and partners run its models on humanoids through an <a href='https://www.pi.website/blog/api-beta'>API</a>. <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6ZlF6QWFPmw'>Sergey Levine's reason</a> for not collecting data on humanoids: they're expensive, complex and hard to teleoperate.</p><p>The <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16054'>π0.5 paper</a> shows what variety buys. About 400 hours came from the target robots in about 100 homes, and those home-task examples were only 2.4% of the first training phase (a share of training examples, not of hours). On unfamiliar objects the full model succeeds 94% of the time. Take out the data from static arms in homes and it drops to 31%. Take out the other lab robots and it's 49%. Take out web data and it's 74%. The 97.6% that came from other robots and the web carries much of that result.</p><p>The second half of the bet is practice. Suppose our robot misses the towel's corner halfway through a fold. What should it do now? A demonstration of a perfect fold may never show that situation. With <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14759'>RECAP</a>, the robot tries the task, a person corrects it when needed, and the model learns from both its attempts and those corrections. On some of the hardest tasks, throughput more than doubled and the failure rate roughly halved. The <a href='https://www.pi.website/blog/pi07'>π0.7</a> release in April 2026 folds that practice into one general policy that matches specialist RL policies, and <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15483'>its paper</a> reports 60% to 80% success on unseen tasks.</p><figure class='rc-video'><div style='position:relative;width:100%;aspect-ratio:16/9'><iframe style='position:absolute;inset:0;width:100%;height:100%;border:0' src='https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/Zn8yMaepzVk' title='Pi robot manipulation in an unfamiliar home' loading='lazy' allow='accelerometer; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; fullscreen' referrerpolicy='strict-origin-when-cross-origin' allowfullscreen></iframe></div><figcaption>Pi's manipulation demonstration in an unfamiliar home. <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zn8yMaepzVk'>Watch on YouTube</a>.</figcaption></figure><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='transfer' data-view='transfer' data-title='Gain from adding human video'></div><h2 id='sec-transfer'>Human video helps robots without human bodies</h2><p>Back to the question. If the body had to match, human video would help humanoids and do nothing for other robots. It helps both. On Pi's two-arm wheeled robot, <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22414'>3 hours of tracked human demonstrations</a> raised spice-rack success from 32% to 71%, in a kitchen that appeared only in the human demonstrations. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10952'>ImMimic</a> is the cleanest test: the same human videos, one arm, 4 different end effectors. With the ImMimic method, adding human video raised success by about 26 percentage points with grippers and 25 with dexterous hands. Almost the same gain! The method matters, though: simply training on the human and robot data together gave smaller gains. On humanoids, <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13441'>HAT</a> went from 59/170 to 101/170, about +25 points, in the same range.</p><p>The camera and the grasp still matter. In <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06627'>the What Matters study</a>, matching the camera's image scale between human and robot took success from 26.2% to 51.2%, while a badly mismatched camera gave no gain. In <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08436'>EgoWAM</a>, when the human grasp couldn't work with the robot's hand, success went from 25% to 15% over 20 trials, which the authors call within noise.</p><p>So human video helps both kinds of body, at small scale. Nobody knows yet what happens at Figure's scale. In Pi's experiments, human demonstrations helped only after diverse robot pretraining, and Figure's recipe of massive human-only pretraining has no cited test on a robot without a human body. And a few trials can fool us. Suppose one body succeeds 50% of the time and another 80%. Even that large gap takes roughly 40 trials per body to detect reliably (at 5% significance and 80% power), and these studies typically run only 10 to 50. My read is that Figure has strong evidence that human video helps and weak evidence that the humanoid body is necessary. The companion article, <a href='/robotics/do-you-need-a-human-body-for-human-data/'>Do you need a human body for human data?</a>, compares the studies in detail.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='wheels' data-view='wheels' data-title='Reach is a separate question'></div><h2 id='sec-wheels'>Stairs are a separate argument</h2><p>Legs have a second argument, and it's completely separate: stairs. A conventional wheeled base can't climb them and needs room to turn, so in a house with stairs it reaches fewer rooms. That's about where the robot can go, and it says nothing about how fast a body learns from human video. In step-free flats, warehouses and offices with elevators the stairs argument goes away, and cost and manipulation decide. A Roomba only does one floor and it's still a useful product.</p><p>Cost is where the evidence is thinnest. Levine said in a <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=48pxVdmkMIE'>2025 interview</a> that each Pi arm costs about $3,000. Figure hasn't published a price for the Figure 03, and Adcock says the next Figure hand has more actuators than an entire robot. On those numbers I lean toward a cheaper wheeled manipulator in step-free homes and a humanoid where the job needs stairs.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='ceilings' data-view='ceilings' data-title='Limits, and what we can measure'></div><h2 id='sec-ceilings'>Nobody has measured the nines yet</h2><p>It's tempting to rank them, and you can't. They answer four different questions with different tasks and metrics, and they're company or lab reports.</p><p>Then there's resolution. If a robot fails zero times in n trials, the 95% upper bound on its failure rate is about 3/n. To show a failure rate under 0.1%, you need about 3,000 trials in a row without a failure. For our chore robot that's two months of flawless work, and no published evaluation is large enough to establish 99.9%. The jump from 8% to 56% is the early part of the curve. The hard part is getting rid of the failures that happen once in a thousand chores. If our demonstrations never show the robot missing a towel corner and recovering, copying successful folds gives it little direct guidance for that mistake. This is why I want to see what a robot learns from its own failed attempts.</p><p>For our chore robot, the questions are concrete: can it reach the towel, does its training show how to grasp it, and can it recover when the grasp goes wrong? If I were building it, I'd start from the product. Does the job need stairs, and how many failures a month will the owner accept? Those two answers choose the hardware. After that, I'd choose Pi's correction loop: turn failures in real homes into the next training examples, as fast as possible.</p><details><summary>Sources, company background and how the numbers were computed</summary><p><strong>Numbers.</strong> Failure budget: 50 tasks/day × 30 days = 1,500 tasks/month; one failure a month on average gives a failure probability of about 1/1,500, so success ≈ 99.93%. Zero failures in n trials gives an approximate 95% upper bound of 3/n on the failure rate, so a 0.1% bound needs about 3,000 failure-free trials; the source gives no full sampling model. About 40 trials per body detect a 30-point difference from a 50% baseline at 5% significance and 80% power; the statistical procedure was not specified. Figure's report text gives a 9% baseline; its displayed counts give 35/420 ≈ 8%.</p><p><strong>Figure's hardware.</strong> Figure builds its actuators, motors, sensors, battery pack and electronics in house.
