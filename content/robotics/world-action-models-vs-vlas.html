Put a camera over a crumpled towel, type “fold the towel”, and ask a network for the next few dozen motor commands. Two families of models now compete for that job, and from the outside you can't tell them apart (section ① of the board). A vision-language-action model (VLA) like π0.5 starts from a backbone pretrained on images paired with text, so it arrives already knowing what a towel is. A world action model (WAM) like Fast-WAM starts from a video generator instead. The bet is that video pretraining teaches how scenes change more directly than image–text pretraining does.

The word “world” should make you a little suspicious. Suppose the robot has to generate a video of the towel folding before it can move. Now every decision pays for making that video! Some WAMs work exactly like this. Motus generates the frames and the motor commands together, and LingBot-VA generates the frames first, then works out the commands that connect them. Each video step is a full pass through a big network, and LingBot-VA on RoboTwin takes 25 of them for every chunk of actions. But wait. If predicting video is what helps the robot learn, do we really have to keep generating video after training? Those are two separate switches: train on future frames or not, and draw them while the robot runs or not. Fast-WAM's authors flipped both on one model, and the board follows that experiment.

So which family wins? Section ④ says it depends on the scoreboard, and the scoreboards don't share a footing. Besides the robot-pretraining and compilation gaps printed under the table, the baseline success scores come from separate papers, so a higher number doesn't tell you which part of the system earned it. Robustness is the strangest scoreboard. What happens if you change the background or the lighting, or put unfamiliar objects in view? One study kept the backbone, data and training recipe fixed and found that a variant generating video along with its actions held up better than Fast-WAM: 55.6% versus 45.6% success on a real robot, and 66.3% versus 49.1% on LIBERO-Plus. So drawing frames buys robustness? Then Faster-WAM, which never draws a frame, reaches 78.3% on LIBERO-Plus, and the results stop pointing one way. (Careful with that name: the study I just cited, 2608.04404, is a different August 2026 paper that is also called Faster-WAM.)

Even the label is soft. A June survey calls a model a WAM whenever predicted futures help produce, score or train its actions. Under that definition, starting from a language model doesn't rule a system out. Nor does skipping video generation while the robot runs: Fast-WAM still counts, because its action predictions use features trained to predict the future. But adding a future-prediction exercise to a VLA isn't enough by itself. The branches are also older than the name. GR-1 (2023) learned future images alongside actions, and VPP and UVA used video features without drawing frames, so the field never marched in a straight line from slow to fast. The survey's own complaint is that the name spreads faster than the idea gets clear. “World action model” went from 12 arXiv matches in 2026 Q1 to 214 in Q3 (“vision language action”: 357 to 646), and phrase counts like these miss LingBot-VA and catch some driving papers. What I find most useful here is that learning to predict video and generating it while the robot runs are separate choices, and Fast-WAM's experiment prices them separately. So when you compare two of these policies, check what each one predicts during training and what it generates at run time, then compare them on the task, the distribution shifts and the action horizon your robot needs.
