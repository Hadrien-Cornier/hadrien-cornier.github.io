Reading a clean explanation of Q-learning feels a lot like understanding it. Can you predict what a broken learner will do? In Breaking Bellman, you predict, watch it train in your browser for a few seconds, then repair its update rule by swapping words. There are 17 levels, playable in any order. Menu repairs are easier than inventing PPO, but you still have to find the broken word.

Take CartPole: push a cart left or right to keep its pole upright. The network's guess of future discounted reward is called Q. One run reached 4,700,000 and was still climbing. Hmm. Each step pays 1, each later reward counts 0.99 as much, and episodes stop after 200 steps. Before looking below, pick the largest possible discounted return: (a) 200, (b) about 86.6, (c) unlimited.

Show the return answer

Answer: (b). Add 200 terms: 1 + 0.99 + 0.99² + … = (1 − 0.99²⁰⁰)/(1 − 0.99) ≈ 86.6. Choice (a) is the undiscounted score ceiling. Choice (c) would require both no discount and no time limit. Even with unlimited time, this discount caps the return at 100.

A stable learner can still learn the wrong thing. The bonus level's 2D dog uses PPO to fetch a stick, with +1 for retrieval, −0.01 per step and up to +0.05 per step for facing it. It learned to face the stick and sit, earning about 2.12 per throw. Three times more training made it a better sitter. Let's compare what sitting and fetching actually pay.

Sit or fetch?
  • Assume a 60-step throw and the full 0.05 facing bonus every step.
  • Sit: 60 × (0.05 − 0.01) = 2.4.
  • Hypothetical 15-step fetch: 15 × (0.05 − 0.01) + 1 = 1.6.
  • Sitting wins above a bonus of 0.01 + 1/(60 − 15) = 0.032222… per step; equality gives a tie.
Predict the round trip
No discount; the dog walks 1 m away, then 1 m back. Scheme 1 pays +1 per meter closer and zero for moving away. Scheme 2 pays the change in potential Φ = −distance, at 1 reward unit per meter. What are their total shaping rewards?
  • (a) +1 and 0
  • (b) 0 and 0
  • (c) +1 and +1
Show the shaping answer

Answer: (a). Scheme 1 pays 0 out and +1 back. Scheme 2 pays −1 out and +1 back. Over longer trips, each middle potential is added once and subtracted once, leaving Φ(end) − Φ(start), which is zero on a closed loop. Choice (b) misses scheme 1's free outward movement; (c) misses scheme 2's outward charge.
