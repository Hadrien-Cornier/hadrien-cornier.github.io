robot companies have a neat argument for their shape. There's a mountain of video of people doing chores, and people have two arms, two hands and eyes on top of a body, so a robot built like a person should be the one that learns from all that footage. Figure AI made this case in its <a href='https://www.figure.ai/news/project-go-big'>Project Go-Big</a> announcement (September 2025): a humanlike viewpoint and humanlike kinematics should make everyday video easier to learn from. I like the story, and I wanted to know if anyone had measured it.</p><p>So I went through roughly 30 papers that train robots on human video and asked each one the same thing: when you add the human data, how much better does the robot get, and does that depend on the robot's body? By body I mean everything physical: its geometry, where its cameras sit, which motions it can make and which places it can get into. Roboticists call this the embodiment. Moving a skill from one body to another (yours to a robot's, say) is cross-embodiment transfer.</p><p>But what should the robot actually copy? Your fingers bend differently from its fingers, your camera moves with your head, and the video never says how hard you squeezed the cup. Somehow that footage has to become actions the robot's own body can execute.</p><p>The usual recipe goes like this. A person wears a head-mounted camera (often Meta's Aria glasses) and does the task with their hands in view. Software estimates the 3D motion of their hands in every frame. Then we train the model that picks the robot's next action (the policy) on these recordings plus demonstrations performed with the robot itself. The number to watch is the gain: the score with human data minus the score without it, with the robot data held the same. A gain of +20 means the videos bought 20 percentage points.</p><span id="section-count-the-nines-before-you-pick-the-body" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-human-data-helps-bodies-that-look-nothing-like-us" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-same-human-data-different-hands" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-what-breaks-transfer-is-mismatch" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-what-carries-over-and-what-doesn-t" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-wheels-in-a-flat-with-no-stairs" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><div class='rc-mark' id='sec-same' data-panel='same' data-view='same' data-title='Same videos, four hands'></div><h2>Same 100 videos, four different hands</h2><p>Let's start with the experiment that tests the humanoid argument most directly: keep the human data fixed and change only the body. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.10952'>ImMimic</a> does exactly that. One Franka arm, the same 100 human videos, four tasks, and four different things bolted to the end of the arm: two parallel grippers (Robotiq and Fin Ray) and two dexterous hands (Allegro and Ability). The hands are the most human thing in the room. If humanlike fingers improve transfer, the hands should gain more from the same videos, each measured against its own robot-only baseline.</p><p>They don't. Grouped, the grippers go from 53.75% to 80%, a gain of 26.25 points, and the hands go from 42.5% to 67.5%, a gain of 25. The extra fingers got the same lift out of the same videos. (Per device, the final scores are 72.5% and 87.5% for the grippers, 60% and 75% for the hands.)</p><p>Two other studies swap bodies differently and land in the same place. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11628'>LUCID</a> gives a LEAP hand and a two-finger gripper one hour of phone video per task and no robot demonstrations at all (each body gets its own controller, trained in simulation), and both succeed on 19 of 30 trials. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07607'>EgoVerse</a> comes closest to a whole-body test: two gripper robots and a Unitree G1 humanoid, three labs, one shared human dataset. In new scenes the humanoid's gain lands between the two grippers' gains.</p><p>The catch is sample size. Each ImMimic cell is 10 trials, and at 10 trials a 50% success rate carries a 95% uncertainty of about ±31 points, wide enough to hide a real 25-point gap between bodies. The hand swaps also never test a camera riding on a walking person's head, and the EgoVerse robots had different cameras and some lab-specific data. Put together: across the bodies tested the gains look comparable, nobody has demonstrated a humanoid advantage, and a small one could still be hiding.</p><div class='rc-mark' id='sec-gains' data-panel='gains' data-view='gains' data-title='Every body gets a lift'></div><h2>Every kind of body gets a lift</h2><p>Now zoom out to the reported comparisons on the board. Most compare training with and without human data. Masquerade and HAT also change the training setup, and EMMA compares human video with equal hours of teleoperation. Gripper arms gain, wheeled bases gain, humanoids gain. The biggest gain sits on a plain gripper arm: <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.09976'>Masquerade</a> takes it from 12% to 74% across three tasks in new scenes by training on human videos edited to look like the robot. (The 12% averages three image-encoder baselines, and one of them, HRP, was already pretrained on raw human video.) The largest humanoid evaluation, Figure's <a href='https://www.figure.ai/news/helix-2-5-zero-shot-30-home-generalization'>Helix 2.5</a> report, ran 420 trials per condition in 30 homes it had no training data from. Index pretraining took success from 35 to 237 of those trials, about +48 points. This result comes from a company blog post without peer review.</p><p>So the first answer is clear: you don't need a human-shaped body to profit from human video. What the plot can't do is rank bodies. Every dot comes from a different lab, a different task and often a different score (some are success rates, some partial credit), so the gap between two dots compares two experiments.</p><p>One dot falls below zero. On a grocery-bag task, one EgoVerse gripper robot dropped from a task score of about 0.43 to 0.10 when human data was added. The authors suspect the robot's body pushed it toward a different strategy than the person's. Hold on to that red dot.</p><div class='rc-mark' id='sec-carries' data-panel='carries' data-view='carries' data-title='What a video carries'></div><h2>What a video of someone tidying actually contains</h2><p>How can a gripper learn anything useful from watching a human hand? Split the video into the kinds of information it carries (I'll call them channels) and check which ones each of four kinds of body can use. The board runs from easy at the top to hard at the bottom.</p><p><strong>What and where.</strong> Which objects, what the room looks like, which step comes next. This transfers well and doesn't care what the hand looks like. Suppose a robot has never practiced on a particular spice setup, and only the human videos show it. Can watching a person handle it help? In a <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22414'>Physical Intelligence study</a> with a mobile ARX robot, success rose from 32% to 71%. On egg sorting, where the robot-only model placed eggs at random, accuracy went from 57% to 78%. Training both the step-planning part and the action part of the model on human data beat training either one alone.</p><p><strong>Where to go.</strong> Human walking video helps robots on wheels navigate. A <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01951'>CobotMagic base</a> reaches 9 of 12 unseen start points instead of 5, and <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03603'>LeLaN</a> finds objects 0.89 of the time instead of 0.65 once YouTube and human walking video are added. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10106'>EgoHumanoid</a>'s G1, trained on human data alone, completes the walking substep in all four of its tasks. It does this by turning the person's lower-body motion into speed and stand-or-squat commands for an existing walking controller, so the human data taught it where to walk and the controller supplied the gait.</p><p><strong>Hand path.</strong> <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06994'>MT-π</a> had people close a drawer in a direction the robot had never been shown, and a gripper arm went from 0 of 10 to 18 of 20. That's a movement the robot already had, pointed somewhere new. Can video teach the robot a movement it couldn't already make? None of these studies shows that, and <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08436'>EgoWAM</a>'s authors leave it as an open question.</p><p><strong>Fingers.</strong> Finger motion transfers when the robot has fingers. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16710'>EgoScale</a> gives us a useful test: put a humanoid upper body on wheels, hold the base and torso still, and let the fingers do the work. The robot was a Galaxea R1 Pro with Sharpa hands, each with 22 independently movable joints. After identical robot training, mean success was 0.02 without human pretraining, 0.38 with it, and 0.56 with an additional stage using aligned human and robot play data. Finger-joint labels beat wrist-only labels. The benefit rides on the hands and needs no legs. It does need robot data: a G1 with three-finger hands only gained once G1 play data joined mid-training.</p><p><strong>Contact and precision.</strong> Here the video runs dry. A camera sees fingers close on a cup and records nothing about the squeeze. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20290'>EgoZero</a> reports 1–2 cm error in its video-derived action labels and attributes its high-precision task failure to that error. On EgoHumanoid's most precise substep, human-only training scores 5%, robot-only 15%, and both together 60%. Human video alone scored 0% in <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17759'>MotionTrans</a> and on <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.12440'>EgoVLA</a>'s simulated humanoid.</p><p><strong>Legs.</strong> This is the thinnest evidence. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17820'>CityWalker</a> trains a Go1 quadruped to navigate from 2,000 hours of walking and driving video. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10353'>UMI on Legs</a> reaches 85% on a Go2 with data from a handheld gripper rig. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10454'>HumanPlus</a> makes a humanoid walk and squat from human motion, and <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03729'>VideoMimic</a> gets a real G1 up stairs and into a chair. Great demos, and none of them trains the same robot with and without the human data, so none measures what the video added. No quadruped study I read isolates it.</p><div class='rc-mark' id='sec-fixes' data-panel='fixes' data-view='fixes' data-title='Fix the data'></div><h2>When human data hurts, fix the data</h2><p>Back to the red dot. Why does adding human data sometimes hurt? In the clearest cases, the demonstrations don't fit the robot. EgoWAM has the cleanest example. In a cup-placing task the robot grasps cups from the front, and people naturally grab them from the side. Natural human recordings drop success from the robot-only 25% to 15%. Recordings of people grasping the robot's way raise it to 35% (20 rollouts each).</p><p>The cup is one way recordings can mismatch the robot. The board shows three more, each with a repair: the camera shows objects at the wrong scale (<a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06627'>What Matters</a>), a human hand looks nothing like a gripper (<a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00779'>Phantom</a>), or a walking path needs reshaping for a wheeled base (<a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04443'>EMMA</a>). <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19626'>EgoBridge</a> repairs the model instead: it changes how the model represents human and robot recordings so it can learn from both. ImMimic's alignment step works the same way. Without it, plain joint training gave the grippers only +6.25 points and the hands +16.25.</p><p>Each repair changed the strategy, the camera, the pixels, the path or the representation, and every one left the robot as it was. You can also attack the mismatch from the recording side. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10329'>UMI</a> is a handheld gripper with the robot's own fingers and camera. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03310'>RDT2</a> trained on about 10,000 hours of that kind of data and runs on arms it never saw, with no robot data, as long as the camera and gripper models match. That gives two routes to compatible data: build a robot that matches the human, or build a recorder that matches the robot.</p><p>Two more ingredients carry real weight. Physical Intelligence found that the human-data benefit grows with more diverse pretraining, and that with little or no pretraining the human data didn't help. They treat human-to-robot transfer as the same kind of thing as transfer between two robot bodies, with the person as one more body. And nearly every working result here also trains on robot demonstrations.</p><div class='rc-mark' id='sec-nines' data-panel='nines' data-view='nines' data-title='Counting nines'></div><h2>Ninety percent is the easy part</h2><p>Everything so far lives in the world of ordinary success rates, where going from 30% to 70% is a triumph. A robot in your home has to almost never fail. Count in nines: 90%, 99%, 99.9%. Each nine cuts failures tenfold, and each one is much harder to measure, let alone reach.</p><p>A useful rule: if you run n trials and see zero failures, the 95% upper bound on the failure rate is about 3/n. A perfect 30 of 30 still allows 10% failures, and showing failures under 1% takes about 300 successes in a row. Most comparisons I read use 10 to 40 trials per condition. The best results sit at the left edge of the scale. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24934'>HumanEgo</a>, trained on human data alone, averages 92.5% over four tabletop tasks at 40 trials each, the best task average I found. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15704'>Human0</a>'s jump from 15 of 30 to 30 of 30 on a G1 is a choice between two objects, where guessing gets about 50%. None of these studies establishes 99%.</p><p>What closes the last gap? Physical Intelligence's table-clearing task, with objects missing from the robot demos, gives a hint. The baseline scores about 0.53. Three hours of human data lift it to about 0.63, 7.45 hours from a different robot (a UR5) to about 0.71, and data from the target robot to about 0.88. The durations differ and the human and UR5 error bars overlap, so the firm conclusion is that target-robot data won by a wide margin. (On egg sorting and the dresser task, human data came close to target-robot data.) My guess, and it's only a guess, is that contact and force become the limit as you climb toward 99%, and video doesn't record them. The companion piece on <a href='/robotics/three-robot-philosophies/#sec-nines'>three robot philosophies</a> explains why to count nines first, following Waymo's Dmitri Dolgov.</p><div class='rc-mark' id='sec-body' data-panel='body' data-view='body' data-title='Two levers'></div><h2>What the body decides</h2><p>So there are two levers. Body geometry decides which movements are feasible and which places the robot can physically get to. Data compatibility decides whether a demonstration is usable. Human video, as far as anyone has measured, moves the second lever.</p><p>Figure argues for the first one. Its Helix 2.5 report says a wheeled base needs floor clearance to fit and turn, while a humanoid can position its body where other forms can't. That's plausible in a cramped kitchen and a fine reason to build a humanoid. I found no measurement of it, and it's a claim about reach, separate from learning from video.</p><p>Say you're choosing a robot for a flat with no stairs. I'd lean toward something like the <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16054'>π0.5</a> platform: wheels that can move sideways as well as forward, two arms with parallel-jaw grippers, and a torso that raises and lowers. Wheeled robots show the same range of gains from human video, nobody has shown that humanlike hands extract more, and wheels are presumably cheaper. Nobody has measured that cost, or run the same human data through a full humanoid and a wheeled robot on the same home tasks, and that's the experiment I'd most like to see. Add stairs and the answer changes. Three open questions could move it too: whether contact and force cap reliability on the way to 99%, whether humanoids get around tight homes better, and whether a full-body viewpoint helps once everything else is held fixed.</p><p>If you're building a robot that learns from human video, pick hardware that fits the task and collect varied human video. Fix the mismatches between that video and your robot: strategy, camera, appearance, paths. Pretrain on diverse data, and add experience from the target robot, especially for contact and precision.</p><details><summary>How the gains were measured</summary><p>Gain is the score with human data minus the score without it, normally with identical robot-demonstration data. Where a paper offered it, I used the evaluation whose scenes, objects, tasks or start points only the human demonstrations covered. Otherwise I used the paper's main comparison. Success rates, partial-credit task scores and accuracy stay as different metrics and are not converted into each other.</p><p>Values marked ~ come from reading unnumbered bars in EgoVerse Fig. 9 and Physical Intelligence Figs. 10–11, to about ±2 points. The ±31-point figure uses the normal approximation 1.96 × √(0.25/10) ≈ 0.31, not an exact binomial interval. The 3/n rule is also an approximation. The EgoVerse grocery baseline reads as about 0.43, so the drop is roughly −32.5 points.</p><p>A few dots have conditions of their own. EMMA compares one extra hour of human video with one extra hour of mobile teleoperation (both on top of 50 stationary-base robot demonstrations), so its +30 is relative to equal teleoperation time. EMMA's retargeting result (52% to 82%) is a separate comparison that happens to share those numbers. HAT changes its state representation at the same time as it adds human data.
