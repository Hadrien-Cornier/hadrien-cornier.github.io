aria-live='polite'>1 · Choose, track, look again</div></div><div class='rc-body'><p>Put a red block in front of a small robot arm and ask it to pick the block up. The arm starts reaching, and halfway there someone nudges the block 9 cm to the side. What should the arm do next?</p><p>You already know the answer: look again, and go where the block is now. But “what is the next movement?” is the question robot control has been rebuilt around over the last few years, and answering it well is harder than it sounds. The robot has to answer while the scene changes, while the camera can’t show what already happened, on tasks nobody demonstrated, and with limited time to compute. I want to walk through the handful of ideas that changed how robots answer it, from a 2009 planner to 2026 models that imagine video and keep notes. We’ll keep coming back to the same arm and block, changing the situation each time: a mug in the way, an unfamiliar object, a failed grasp, or something the robot needs to remember.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='loop' data-view='loop' data-title='1 · Choose, track, look again'></div><h2 id='section-before-2023-plan-then-track'>Choosing a movement and following it are two jobs</h2><p>“Move the arm” hides two jobs. One is choosing where the gripper should go next. The other is making the motors get there: a controller compares the commanded joint angles with the measured ones and drives the motors to close the gap, many times a second (I covered that half in <a href='/robotics/so101-1-target-and-goal/'>the SO-101 target and goal post</a>). The nudged block shows why the two jobs have to stay separate. Hand the best tracker in the world the old target and it will follow it perfectly and close the gripper on air, 9 cm from the block. Once the world moves, accurate execution of the old plan is worth nothing. The fix has to happen in the choosing.</p><figure class='rc-media'><video autoplay loop muted playsinline controls preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/observe-again.png'><source src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/observe-again.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/observe-again.mp4'>Open the feedback video</a></video><figcaption>Same reach, block moved about 9 cm. One arm keeps its first observation; the other observes again every 0.1 s. Scripted SO-101 motion, MuJoCo inverse kinematics, Genesis rendering.</figcaption></figure><p>The fix is a loop: observe, choose, let the motors track for a moment, check what happened, observe again. I scripted both arms in the clip, so no trained policy is involved.</p><p>Classical robotics built that loop from hand-designed parts. A <a href='https://www.kavrakilab.org/publications/rusu-sucan2009real-time-perception-guided-motion.pdf#page=7'>2009 PR2 system</a> is a clean picture of it: 3D perception turned camera data into a model of the table and its obstacles, a planner computed a collision-free path, a tracking controller followed the path, and an executive replanned when the measurements changed. It worked, and it also shows where the approach strains. The tested grasps approached horizontally, and planning grasps for general objects from partial views was left for later work. Every new object and every new failure is something an engineer has to anticipate.</p><p>Everything after this replaces the choosing with learning. Watch what stays: the motor layer at the bottom of the board, and the habit of looking again.</p><details class='rc-drawer'><summary>Learned control before 2023</summary><p>Learning entered the loop earlier. <a href='https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/1504.00702v5?page=13'>Work first released in 2015</a> trained a policy that mapped camera images directly to PR2 joint torques. <a href='https://icml.cc/2011/papers/323_icmlpaper.pdf'>PILCO (2011)</a> learned a dynamics model, with its uncertainty, and used it to improve a policy. The first learns what to do; the second learns how the state will change. That split between action and prediction returns with world-action models below.</p></details><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='chunk' data-view='chunk' data-title='2 · Plan 0.5 s ahead, look every 0.1 s'></div><h2 id='section-act-copy-a-person-one-chunk-at-a-time'>Copy a person, one chunk at a time</h2><p>The most direct way around all that engineering is to show the robot what to do. <a href='https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss19/p016.pdf#page=4'>ALOHA (2023)</a> makes showing cheap. A person moves two small leader arms by hand, and two follower arms copy them. The leader joint readings are the labels, and the followers’ cameras and joint sensors are the inputs. That’s an ordinary supervised dataset: images and joint state in, joint positions out.</p><p>The obvious model predicts one action per frame, and it drifts. Each prediction is a little off, so the arm ends up somewhere the person never put it, so the next input looks unfamiliar, so the next prediction is further off. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13705'>ACT</a>, the policy trained on ALOHA data, predicts a whole sequence of future joint positions at once, an action chunk, and a low-level controller tracks it. A chunk is one coherent movement, so there are far fewer places to wander off.</p><p>A long chunk doesn’t mean the robot stops looking for that long. Two different times are involved. Say each chunk holds <i>H</i> = 10 targets, played at <i>f</i> = 20 targets per second, and the robot executes <i>k</i> = 2 of them before it observes again:</p><p class='rc-eq'><i>T</i><sub>chunk</sub> = <i>H</i> / <i>f</i> = 10 / (20 /s) = 0.5 s &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; <i>T</i><sub>react</sub> = <i>k</i> / <i>f</i> = 2 / (20 /s) = 0.1 s</p><p>The horizon says how far ahead the model plans. The reaction interval says how stale the robot’s view can get before it corrects, with computation time on top.</p><div class='rc-quiz so101-widget' data-type='predict' id='RC1' data-answer='b'><p><strong>Predict.</strong> With these numbers, and ignoring computation, how soon can the arm respond to the nudged block?</p><p><button data-choice='a' disabled>0.5 s</button><button data-choice='b' disabled>0.1 s</button><button data-choice='c' disabled>0.05 s</button><button data-choice='d' disabled>2 s</button></p><p class='rc-quiz-out' aria-live='polite'></p><details><summary>Answer</summary><p>0.1 s. The arm sees the moved block at its next observation, which comes after <i>k</i> = 2 targets at 0.05 s each. The 0.5 s horizon only says how far each plan reaches. Computation time adds to this.</p></details></div><p>Because a new chunk starts every 0.1 s and each covers 0.5 s, several chunks predict the same instant. <a href='https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss19/p016.pdf#page=5'>ACT averages those overlapping predictions</a> (the board shows three meeting at <i>t</i> = 0.2 s), so the motion stays smooth and one bad prediction gets diluted by its neighbors.</p><p>With a 10-minute demonstration budget, ACT reached 80 to 90% success on six dexterous tasks. Mobile ALOHA put the rig on a wheeled base and learned household skills from roughly 50 demonstrations per task, mixed with the stationary ALOHA data. The limit is scope: each ACT model is trained for one task, one setup and one robot.</p><div class='rc-pair'><figure class='rc-media'><video autoplay loop muted playsinline controls preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/action-chunks.png'><source src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/action-chunks.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/action-chunks.mp4'>Open the action-chunk video</a></video><figcaption>Ten target markers per chunk; the arm executes two, then a fresh chunk replaces the rest. Put a mug between the gripper and the block. In half the demonstrations the person goes around the left, in the other half around the right, and both are fine. Now train a network with squared error to output the action for this image. Squared error is minimized by the mean, and the mean of left and right goes straight through the mug.</p><p>The model has to pick one route and commit. <a href='https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss19/p026.pdf#page=3'>Diffusion Policy (2023)</a> borrows the trick image generators use: start from random noise shaped like an action sequence and remove the noise in steps, conditioned on the observation. Different starting noise lands on different routes, and each sample is one whole route. Flow matching does something similar: starting from a noisy action sequence, it learns which direction to change each number to turn that noise into a movement. It can often do this in fewer steps.</p><p>Hold on to one cost, because it comes back in the practice section. A classifier gives you the probability of every action for free. A diffusion or flow model gives you a sample, with no simple formula for how likely that sample was.</p><figure class='rc-media'><img src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v2/candidate-paths-chalk.png' alt='Two valid detours around an obstacle and their average path, which passes through the obstacle.' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Two valid routes and the average that collides with the obstacle.</figcaption></figure><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='vla' data-view='vla' data-title='4 · Borrow meaning from the web'></div><h2 id='section-from-vlm-to-vla-borrow-what-the-web-knows'>Borrow what the web knows</h2><p>Now change the instruction to “put the toy dinosaur in the bin”, with a dinosaur the robot has never seen. Ten minutes of block demonstrations know nothing about dinosaurs. A model trained on a huge pile of captioned web images knows exactly what one looks like.</p><p>So start from that model. A large language model (LLM) maps text to text. A vision-language model (VLM) maps an image plus text to text. A vision-language-action model (VLA) maps a camera image and an instruction, often with the robot’s joint state, to an action. Web pretraining supplies the meaning, and robot demonstrations, still required, connect that meaning to motion.</p><p><a href='https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2307.15818v1?page=5'>RT-2 (2023)</a> made the connection with a neat trick. It cut each action dimension into 256 bins and wrote actions as tokens, so the VLM could say an action the way it says a word. On unseen objects, backgrounds and environments it succeeded 62% of the time, against 32% for the robot-only RT-1 (<a href='https://deepmind.google/blog/rt-2-new-model-translates-vision-and-language-into-action/'>DeepMind’s summary</a>). <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09246'>OpenVLA (2024)</a> makes this recipe available with open weights: image encoders feed a language model whose output tokens specify movement and gripper control.</p><figure class='rc-media'><img src='/assets/robotics/why-robotics/openvla-architecture-chalk.png' alt='OpenVLA architecture: DINOv2 and SigLIP image encoders, an MLP projector, Llama 2 7B and action tokens decoded to a seven-dimensional action.' loading='lazy'><figcaption>OpenVLA: two image encoders (DINOv2 and SigLIP) feed Llama 2 7B, whose tokens decode to a seven-number action for translation, rotation and the gripper.</figcaption></figure><p>Tokens have a price. 256 bins limit precision, and tokens come out one after another, which is slow when the arm wants 50 commands a second. The alternative is an action expert: a separate head that generates a continuous chunk with flow matching. It’s precise and fast, and it carries the cost from the last section: no easy probability. Tokens, clumsy as they are, give a probability for every action. That tradeoff decides which kinds of practice each model can use.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='generalist' data-view='generalist' data-title='5 · One policy, many bodies'></div><h2 id='section-generalist-models-one-policy-for-many-robots'>One policy for many robots</h2><p>Physical Intelligence’s π models show what happens when the robot data gets broad. <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24164'>π0 (October 2024)</a> put a flow-matching action expert on a pretrained VLM and trained it on <a href='https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss21/p010.pdf#page=4'>single-arm, two-arm and mobile robots</a> at once, with tasks like folding laundry, clearing tables and building boxes.</p><p><a href='https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2504.16054v1?page=5'>π0.5 (April 2025)</a> adds a step I like a lot. Before moving, the model writes the next subtask in words (“pick up the plate”), then generates the movement for it, so the same network that executes the task also breaks it into pieces. Trained on web data mixed with data from many robots, it worked in homes it had never seen.</p><p><a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15483'>π0.7 (April 2026)</a> changes what the model is told about each demonstration. Examples carry tags for speed, quality and errors, and optionally an image of the subgoal. Failed and slow attempts can then stay in the data, because the model learns what a bad attempt looks like, and at run time you ask for the good, fast version. PI reports that it makes espresso without task-specific adaptation at roughly the level of specialists tuned with RL; that’s their comparison, and no score is given.</p><p>The bottom of the board hasn’t moved. <a href='https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14759v1#S5.SS1'>π*0.6 outputs joint and gripper targets at 50 Hz</a>. <a href='https://arxiv.org/html/2604.15483v2#S8'>π0.7 outputs joint targets or gripper poses</a>; poses go through inverse kinematics to become joint targets, and a PD controller turns joint targets into motor effort. A smarter decision layer tells you nothing about how well that motor loop tracks.</p><div class='rc-pair'><figure class='rc-media'><iframe src='https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/a6Ix6Vzuk0c' title='π0 capability demonstration' loading='lazy' allow='fullscreen; picture-in-picture' allowfullscreen referrerpolicy='strict-origin-when-cross-origin'></iframe><figcaption>π0, from Physical Intelligence. <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a6Ix6Vzuk0c'>Watch on YouTube</a>.</figcaption></figure><figure class='rc-media'><iframe src='https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/Zn8yMaepzVk' title='π0.5 in an unfamiliar home' loading='lazy' allow='fullscreen; picture-in-picture' allowfullscreen referrerpolicy='strict-origin-when-cross-origin'></iframe><figcaption>π0.5 in a home it had not seen. <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zn8yMaepzVk'>Watch on YouTube</a>.</figcaption></figure></div><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='practice' data-view='practice' data-title='6 · Practice with a critic'></div><h2 id='section-practice-reinforcement-learning-on-real-robots'>Practice: learn from what actually happened</h2><p>Demonstrations often omit the states a robot reaches after its own mistakes, leaving it without examples of how to recover. <a href='https://pd-perry.github.io/posts/post-training.html'>Dong and Finn</a> argue that more imitation doesn’t fix this. The alternative is what you do when you learn a skill: try, see how it went, do more of what worked. That’s reinforcement learning (RL).</p><p>On a real robot, RL is painful, and it’s worth feeling why. Say our arm sends a seven-number command every 20 ms, so one attempt can be about 500 commands followed by one outcome: block in the bin or not. Which of the 500 deserves the blame? Each attempt costs real seconds, and afterwards someone has to put the block back, or step in before something goes badly wrong. The same command can succeed once and fail the next time because the block slid differently. And the commands are continuous, which turns out to be the nastiest part.</p><p>The standard tool is a critic, <i>Q</i>(<i>s</i>, <i>a</i>), a network that estimates how good action <i>a</i> is in state <i>s</i>. Given a critic, acting well means picking</p><p class='rc-eq'><i>a</i>* = argmax<sub><i>a</i></sub> <i>Q</i>(<i>s</i>, <i>a</i>)</p><p>With a few discrete actions you check them all. With seven continuous numbers, even a coarse grid is hopeless.</p><div class='rc-quiz so101-widget' data-type='predict' id='RC2' data-answer='b'><p><strong>Predict.</strong> Seven action dimensions, 20 bins each. How many candidates does the critic have to score for one decision?</p><p><button data-choice='a' disabled>140</button><button data-choice='b' disabled>1.28 billion</button><button data-choice='c' disabled>7<sup>20</sup></button><button data-choice='d' disabled>20</button></p><p class='rc-quiz-out' aria-live='polite'></p><details><summary>Answer</summary><p>20<sup>7</sup> = 1,280,000,000. Each of the 7 dimensions takes one of 20 values independently, so the counts multiply. At 50 decisions a second, checking them all is out.</p></details></div><p>Instead of checking every command, we can train another network to propose one. This is the actor. The critic tells it how a small change would improve the score, so training pushes its proposals uphill. (DDPG, TD3 and SAC use versions of this idea.) Climbing gets stuck on the nearest hill. The VLA proposes <i>N</i> action chunks, a small edit actor adds a bounded change to each, and the critic picks the best of the originals and the edits:</p><p class='rc-eq'><i>a</i>* = argmax<sub><i>a</i> ∈ {<i>a</i><sup><i>i</i></sup>, <i>a</i><sup><i>i</i></sup> + <i>â</i><sup><i>i</i></sup>}</sub> <i>Q</i>(<i>s</i>, <i>a</i>), &nbsp; with ‖<i>â</i><sup><i>i</i></sup>‖ ≤ ε</p><p>The robot executes the winner, and the improved behavior is later trained back into the VLA. PPO, the usual on-policy method, needs the ratio of an action’s probability under the new and old policy; this only needs the critic’s score.</p><p>On eight tasks with 30 trials each (egg handling, billiards and flower insertion among them), imitation fine-tuning averaged 20.5/30 and human-corrected imitation (HG-DAgger) 22.1/30. Thirty lab trials per task measure a lab result; deployment reliability needs many more.</p><figure class='rc-media'><img src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v3/rl-finetune-results.png' alt='Bar chart of successes out of 30 on eight tasks for imitation fine-tuning, HG-DAgger and EXPO-FT.' loading='lazy'><figcaption>EXPO-FT Table 2, redrawn: successes out of 30 per task.</figcaption></figure><details class='rc-drawer'><summary>The EXPO-FT dataflow, HIL-SERL and RLT</summary><figure class='rc-media'><img src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v3/expo-ft-architecture-chalk.png' alt='EXPO-FT dataflow: VLA proposals, bounded edits, critic selection and training back into the VLA.' loading='lazy'></figure><p><a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21845'>HIL-SERL (Berkeley, 2024)</a> starts from a few demonstrations, trains a small policy with SAC-style actor-critic learning, and lets a person take over with a joystick when things go wrong; the takeovers become training data. It reports up to 3× speed on the hardest segment of a task after minutes to a few hours of practice.</p></details><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='advantage' data-view='advantage' data-title='7 · Credit inside a failed attempt'></div><h2 id='section-credit-inside-a-failed-attempt'>Credit inside a failed attempt</h2><p><a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14759'>π*0.6 (PI, 2025)</a> goes after the 500-command blame problem with a method called RECAP. It judges segments of an attempt instead of whole attempts. A value function <i>V</i>(<i>o</i>) estimates the future reward from observation <i>o</i>. In our example each step costs −1, so <i>V</i> reads as minus the expected number of steps left. For a segment of <i>N</i> steps starting at step <i>t</i>, <a href='https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14759v1#S4'>the advantage</a> is</p><p class='rc-eq'><i>Â</i><sub><i>t</i></sub> = Σ<sub><i>k</i>=<i>t</i></sub><sup><i>t</i>+<i>N</i>−1</sup> <i>r</i><sub><i>k</i></sub> + <i>V</i>(<i>o</i><sub><i>t</i>+<i>N</i></sub>) − <i>V</i>(<i>o</i><sub><i>t</i></sub>)</p><p>Take <i>N</i> = 10 steps, all rewarded −1, with <i>V</i> = −40 at the start and −25 at the end: <i>Â</i> = −10 + (−25) − (−40) = +5. The segment spent 10 steps and brought the robot 15 steps closer to done, which is good progress. With a threshold of +2 it gets labeled good, even if the block later slips and the attempt fails.</p><div class='rc-quiz so101-widget' data-type='predict' id='RC3' data-answer='b'><p><strong>Predict.</strong> The attempt failed. One of its segments has advantage +5, and the threshold is +2. What happens to that segment in training?</p><p><button data-choice='a' disabled>Thrown away with the failed attempt</button><button data-choice='b' disabled>Trained as a good example</button><button data-choice='c' disabled>Trained as a bad example</button><button data-choice='d' disabled>Ignored until the task succeeds</button></p><p class='rc-quiz-out' aria-live='polite'></p><details><summary>Answer</summary><p>It trains as a good example. The label comes from the segment’s advantage, +5 &gt; +2, and the episode’s final outcome doesn’t override it. The policy learns this behavior under the “good” label.</p></details></div><p>The policy then trains on images, actions and these labels, and at run time you always ask for good. It’s the π0.7 tagging idea with a critic writing the tags, and it needs no action probability. PI reports more than 2× the throughput and roughly half the failures on selected hard tasks. Every attempt needs a success judgment from a person or a trained detector, a scene reset, a rule for when a human steps in, and sensible hyperparameters. Nobody has a standard complete recipe for those yet.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='world' data-view='world' data-title='8 · Imagine the next frames'></div><h2 id='section-world-action-models-imagine-the-video-and-the-action-together'>Imagine the next frames, then act</h2><p>Back to the arm, about to close on the block. It would help to know what happens if it does: will the block slide away? A policy that only outputs actions never has to answer that. A world-action model does. Give <a href='https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15922'>DreamZero (February 2026)</a> the recent camera frames, the instruction and the robot’s current state. It starts with noisy video and noisy actions, then refines both together: a guess at what the arm will do and what the camera will see. It executes the actions, and then the real frames replace the imagined ones as context.</p><p>That replacement is the feedback loop from the first section again. A model that keeps conditioning on its own imagined frames compounds its errors; swapping in measurements clears the accumulated drift every cycle. The coupling also cuts the other way. In DreamZero’s failure examples, when the imagined video leaves out the drawing on a whiteboard, the robot doesn’t draw it, and when the imagined pastry scene drifts, the execution fails. Bad imagination and bad action show up together; from examples alone you can’t tell which causes which, or how often.</p><p>The payoff is transfer. The authors report more than 2× the generalization of leading VLAs on new tasks and environments. 10 to 20 minutes of action-free video of another robot or of a human gave more than 42% relative improvement on an unseen task, and 30 minutes of play data adapted it to a new robot while keeping its zero-shot behavior.</p><p>The bill is latency. The backbone has 14 billion parameters, and <a href='https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15922v1'>the first version took 5.7 s per action step</a>. Optimizations brought that to 150 ms on an NVIDIA GB200, 38× faster, or about 7 Hz. DreamZero-Flash trains on heavily corrupted video so that one denoising step already gives useful actions while the video stays coarse. I read that as a sign that coarse future structure is useful; how much predicted detail control needs remains open. Generating only the actions saves little at this scale, because the number of denoising steps and the network depth dominate the cost.</p><p>Another route skips pixels. <a href='https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2506.09985v1?page=10'>V-JEPA 2 (Meta, 2025)</a> predicts in a learned feature space. To plan, it imagines where each candidate action sequence leads, compares the predicted end features with the features of a goal image, executes the first action of the best sequence and replans. Features are cheaper than pixels, and the search remains: 800 sampled sequences and ten refinement rounds took <a href='https://www.alphaxiv.org/pdf/2506.09985v1?page=15'>16 seconds on one GPU</a>. I wrote more about world models in <a href='/robotics/why-i-am-learning-robotics/'>why I am learning robotics</a>.</p><details class='rc-drawer'><summary>DreamZero figures, predecessors and a leaderboard snapshot</summary><figure class='rc-media'><img src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v3/dreamzero-architecture-chalk.png' alt='DreamZero: joint video and action denoising with a causal video transformer during training, with real frames replacing predictions at inference.' loading='lazy'></figure><figure class='rc-media'><img src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v3/dreamzero-real-vs-generated.png' alt='Predicted video beside observed robot behavior on unfamiliar tasks.' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Predicted and observed behavior on unfamiliar tasks, including cymbals and cooking. Now look at the scene: a block on a table, identical to the scene before the inspection. Inspect, or put it away? The current image can’t tell you. The robot’s situation is partially observable, and so is “did I already add the seasoning?”, “how many have I counted?” and anything now hidden behind something else.</p><figure class='rc-media'><video autoplay loop muted playsinline controls preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/history.png'><source src='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/history.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/modern-control/v4/history.mp4'>Open the history video</a></video><figcaption>Same starting image, two histories: inspection pending or done, so the right next step differs.
