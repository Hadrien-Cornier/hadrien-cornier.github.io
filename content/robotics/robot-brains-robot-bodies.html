Suppose you need a robot to sort cups. A frontier model can do the job, and so can an open model you can download. (Open robot models already exist: OpenVLA builds robot actions on a pretrained vision-language model and releases its weights and training code.) The frontier model may be much smarter. For this job, though, what would you pay extra for? Running a model at a fixed benchmark score also got much cheaper, and Stanford’s 2025 AI Index reports a large drop. If several models sort cups reliably and you can switch between them, I’d expect the suppliers to have a hard time charging much extra. A frontier premium has to come from handling strange exceptions, needing less supervision, or doing work nobody could automate before.

Okay, suppose the model knows which cup you want. How does it close the fingers without dropping it? Understanding the request and controlling the hand are different problems. The second one also depends on the hand, its sensors, the contact and the controller. I expect general models to bring more of the understanding and planning, with robot-specific training connecting them to action. π0.5 already mixes web knowledge with experience from several robot bodies to work in homes it never saw. So what is a better brain worth on the cup line? Let’s buy it two upgrades and price them.

Panel C is the number I’d buy on, and it points to a new kind of seller. You can rent someone else’s GPUs instead of buying your own. The owner buys the machines, makes them usable and needs enough paying customers to keep them busy, and that takes serious capital (CoreWeave’s GPU-backed financing is one example). Now imagine doing that with robots. A fleet operator buys the arms, installs them at your site and charges per cup sorted. Buying the robots is only the start. Can the operator keep them in paid work long enough to cover the purchase, the repairs and the risk that a better robot makes its fleet hard to rent?

I’m unsure that a big pile of fleet recordings stays exclusive. DeXtreme moved dexterous manipulation from simulation to a real hand on a bounded task. If transfer gets broader and more reliable, rivals can make useful training experience without a big fleet. The worn-finger recording in panel D keeps its value because it shows where the simulator is wrong. Bodies need more than a good design, and the IEA finds some critical-mineral supply chains concentrated (new designs can use less of those minerals). I don’t think this leaves one obvious winner. A model supplier might get paid for hard jobs, a fleet operator for keeping robots working, and a manufacturer for machines other people still wait for. If brains and bodies both get cheap, competition can pass much of the gain to customers as lower prices. For any robotics business, ask which input gets abundant next, and which of its advantages depends on that input staying scarce.
