The clip is 88 seconds of motion played at 4× speed, recorded on 2026-10-11, with the pen taped to the gripper.</p><figure class='ph-wide'><video src='/assets/robotics/so101-series/arm-drawing-overhead.mp4' poster='/assets/robotics/so101-series/arm-drawing-overhead-poster.jpg' autoplay muted loop playsinline controls preload='metadata'></video><figcaption>The real arm traces a kitten on the tablet, filmed from above at 4× speed. <a href='/assets/robotics/so101-series/arm-drawing-overhead.mp4'>Open the video file</a>.</figcaption></figure><p>I want drawings like this to be a measurement. This post belongs to <a href='/robotics/so101-1-target-and-goal/'>a series on the SO-101</a>, and in <a href='/robotics/so101-4-learning-what-physics-misses/'>part 4</a> I built a model of the arm’s joint errors. Ink is the cheapest way to check that model: command a path, let the arm draw it, and compare the line on paper with the line you asked for. That only works if the pen holds still relative to the gripper. We’ll build a printed holder to keep the pen still, then separate what the first print tells us from what we still need to measure. It took five designs, and each of the first four failed for a reason worth knowing.</p><span id="section-design-1-the-pen-on-the-roll-axis" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-design-2-the-pen-beside-the-fingers" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-design-3-two-v-clamps-on-one-part" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-design-4-and-the-last-choice" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-design-5-the-sleeve-on-the-stock-finger" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-every-way-to-hold-the-pen" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-five-designs" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-the-answer-a-sleeve-on-the-stock-finger" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-the-five-designs-side-by-side" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-what-i-will-test" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-what-the-holder-must-do" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p1' data-view='p1' data-title='1 · The pen is noisier than the arm'></div><h2>The pen is noisier than the arm</h2><p>Think about what a line on paper records. Its error is the sum of four things: how the arm really moved, how the pen shifted in its attachment, how wrong my guess of the tip location is, and whatever the paper does to the tip as it drags. Only the first one is the signal.</p><p>The tape setup makes a bad ruler. The gripper is taped shut with its motor disabled, the pen is strapped on, and paper drag can shift it. I can use the recorded motion to estimate where the tip sits relative to the gripper. But even after choosing the position that best matches the data, the mismatch is 3.0 mm. That remaining mismatch is the fit residual. The model predicts about 1.2 mm RMS of drawing error from the arm, so the noise from the pen setup is bigger than the signal I’m trying to see. Compare the first two bars on the board.</p><p>To set targets I need a scale. At a typical pose, with the tip 240 mm in front of the base, an error of 1 mrad on every joint (a milliradian, about 0.06°) moves the tip about 0.3 mm. That’s the size of thing the drawings should resolve. So I asked the holder for:</p><ul><li>less than 0.1 mm of tip play when the paper pushes with 0.5 N,</li><li>less than 0.2 mm of spread when you take the pen out and put it back,</li><li>a tip position known from the geometry, close to the <code>wrist_roll</code> axis,</li><li>support for ink pens and for the Wacom pen (the tablet has to detect it),</li><li>about the mass of the old pen and strap, because in the model 10 g more at the end changes shoulder sag by 2–4 mrad,</li><li>a small print, with nothing to buy and no changes to the arm.</li></ul><p>I searched GitHub, the official SO-ARM100 repository, Printables, Thingiverse, MakerWorld, Cults3D and the LeRobot forum. I found plenty of grippers and camera mounts and no pen holder. Let’s build one.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p2' data-view='p2' data-title='2 · Clearance becomes tip swing'></div><h2>Design 1 puts the pen on the roll axis</h2><p>The cleanest place for a pen is right on the <code>wrist_roll</code> axis, between the fingers. Two nice things happen. The tip location collapses to one unknown, its distance along the axis. And paper drag pushes straight through the axis, so it can’t twist the roll joint.</p><p>The pen also has to follow the paper up and down, so I put it on a carriage that slides on two steel shafts with bronze bushings and rests on the paper under its own weight. A sliding fit needs a little clearance, and clearance means play. When the drag reverses, the pen tips from touching one side of the bushings to touching the other. The tip hangs below the lower bushing, so it swings further than the gap itself:</p><p class='rc-eq'>Δ<sub>tip</sub> = c (1 + 2d / L)</p><p>Here <em>c</em> is the diametral clearance, <em>L</em> is the spacing between the two bushings, and <em>d</em> is how far the tip hangs below the lower one. Ground 3 mm shafts with sized bushings have c between 0.004 and 0.020 mm, and the board works through a typical case: about 0.025 mm at the tip. The estimate I carried for the real part, 0.016–0.049 mm, sits above what this formula gives for the same clearances. Either way it stays well under 0.1 mm. The cost was about 20 parts, a separate printed sleeve for each pen, and two holes drilled into a finger.</p><details><summary>Where c(1 + 2d/L) comes from</summary><p>Put the lower bushing at height 0 and the upper one at height L. With the pen pressed one way at the top and the other way at the bottom, its centre line sits at +c/2 at the lower bushing and −c/2 at the upper one. Extend that straight line a distance d below the lower bushing and it lands at c/2 + (c/L)·d. Reverse the drag and everything mirrors to −c/2 − (c/L)·d. The difference is c + 2cd/L = c(1 + 2d/L). The 1 is the gap at the bushing itself, and 2d/L is the tilt, amplified by the overhang.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/lever.svg' alt='Pen in two bushings leaning to opposite walls as drag reverses, with the tip swing equation' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Clearance reversal in the two-bushing slide. The clearance is drawn 250× larger than it is.</figcaption></figure></details><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p3' data-view='p3' data-title='3 · Fits the gripper, misses the paper'></div><h2>It fit the gripper and missed the paper</h2><p>Design 1 passed every collision check around the gripper. Then I checked it on the whole arm, and it couldn’t reach paper at the level of the base. The pen puts the tip 196 mm below the gripper. To point all that down at the paper, the wrist has to bend further than <code>wrist_flex</code> allows. The smallest fix is to raise the base at least 30 mm.</p><p>Even with the base raised, a long tool costs accuracy, and the reason is a lever. <code>shoulder_lift</code>, <code>elbow_flex</code> and <code>wrist_flex</code> all turn about horizontal axes. If one of them turns by a small angle δθ, the tip moves along the reach by about δθ times the height of that joint above the tip. So 1 mrad at 300 mm of height is 0.3 mm. A long pen under the gripper makes every one of those heights bigger. With a 60 mm riser, design 1 gives 0.42 mm of tip error for 1 mrad on every joint at 240 mm reach.</p><p>This was the lesson of the first design for me. Fitting the gripper is a local check, and it was the easy one. Reach, joint limits, clearance to the paper and the joint-to-tip levers all move together, and you only see them on the whole arm.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p4' data-view='p4' data-title='4 · Seven layouts'></div><h2>Seven places to put a pen</h2><p>So I wrote a search over layouts. For each one, the script pulls the tip as close to the gripper as it can go without the fingers touching the paper or the pen touching the gripper. Then it asks two separate questions. First, with the pen held vertical, which of 144 sampled combinations of forward reach and paper height can the arm reach? Second, at our reference pose (tip 240 mm forward, 1 mrad of error on every joint), how far does the tip move toward or away from the base? It answers the second question once with the paper at base level and once with the base raised 60 mm.</p><p>Two results go against the obvious guess. First, putting the tip near the pinch (the point between the fingertips) doesn’t give the shortest lever. The fingers stick out past the pinch, so a tip right there would put the fingers on the paper. P60, which goes through the pinch at 60°, has the lowest error at the reference pose, but that hand posture reaches far fewer samples. Second, tilting the pen 20° over the back of the fixed finger (Sx20) demands almost half a turn of wrist roll, past the joint limit. It cannot reach the reference pose 240 mm forward, either at base level or with a 60 mm riser.</p><p>I picked Sy20, tilted 20° toward the open-jaw side. It reaches 121 of the 144 sampled reach-and-height combinations, compared with P60’s 78. At 240 mm forward with paper at base level, its predicted error is 0.31 mm versus P60’s 0.26 mm. A pen’s job is to draw shapes across a page, so I’ll trade 0.05 mm at one pose for that much more reach. Every design after this one keeps the Sy20 layout. In this concept the tip sits on the roll axis; in the final part it ends up 2.7–6.0 mm off it, depending on the pen, for a reason we’ll get to.</p><p>One bug I should own up to. An earlier version of the search checked collisions only at mesh corners. It missed the pen passing straight through a flat face of the gripper, which made one layout look better than it was. The search now tests surfaces.</p><details><summary>Full reach and error maps for all seven layouts</summary><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/design1-on-arm.svg' alt='Design 1 on the full arm, unable to reach paper at base level' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Design 1 on the whole arm, drawn with paper contact 200 mm forward.</figcaption></figure><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/all-layouts-1.svg' alt='Axial layout A: concept, pose and reach-error map with shared colour scale' loading='lazy'></figure><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/all-layouts-2.svg' alt='Layouts B0 and Sy10: geometry and reach-error maps' loading='lazy'></figure><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/all-layouts-3.svg' alt='Layouts Sy20 and Sx20: geometry and reach-error maps' loading='lazy'></figure><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/all-layouts-4.svg' alt='Layouts P60 and H: geometry and reach-error maps' loading='lazy'><figcaption>All maps share one scale: reach 100–300 mm, paper height −100 to +100 mm, and colour for tip error from 0.15 to 0.45 mm along the reach, for 1 mrad on every joint. The pen-to-wrist distance is for one pose only.</figcaption></figure></details><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p5' data-view='p5' data-title='5 · Move the give to the paper'></div><h2>Design 2, and moving the give into the paper</h2><p>Design 2 put the sliding carriage on the Sy20 layout. I moved the guide 6 mm outward so it cleared the fingertips, and thickened a narrow section of the support. It came to about 20 parts: a bracket, a carriage, pen sleeves, two ground shafts, four bushings, bolts and glue, with the pen supports 31 mm apart. The play estimate stayed at 0.016–0.049 mm. It also breaks the “nothing to buy” rule, and it’s a lot of hardware for a pen that only needs to go up and down.</p><p>Then I asked why the pen needs to move at all. The slide exists because something has to give when the tip meets the paper, and that something can be the paper. Fix the pen rigidly, put a 3 mm pad under the paper, and command the tip 2 mm into the pad. The shafts, the bushings and the clearance that flips with drag all disappear. The price is that contact force now depends on tip height, because the pad pushes back harder the deeper the tip goes. The Wacom pen senses pressure, so that goes on the test list.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p6' data-view='p6' data-title='6 · A V and a screw'></div><h2>A V and a screw hold any pen from 8 to 13 mm</h2><p>A fixed pen still needs a clamp, and a clamp with a sliding fit brings back the clearance problem. The answer is a 90° V with a screw on the far side. The screw pushes the pen into the V, the pen touches the V along two lines, and there is only one place it can sit. Two of these clamps, spaced along the pen, fix its axis, so a round pen from 8 to 13 mm seats the same way sideways every time.</p><p>The catch is that the seat depends on the diameter. A thinner pen drops deeper into the V. Relative to a 13 mm pen, its axis moves toward the gripper by √2 × (6.5 mm − r), where r is the pen radius. The board projects that shift into the MuJoCo <code>gripper</code> frame, giving each pen its own tool coordinates. These come from CAD, and the V only fixes the pen sideways. How far along its length the pen goes in after reinsertion is a separate number that I haven’t measured.</p><p>The screws are printed too. Why M8 and not something small like M3? At 0.2 mm layers, M3’s 0.5 mm pitch gives 2.5 layers per thread turn, too coarse a staircase to print a usable thread. M8’s 1.25 mm pitch gives 6.25 layers per turn, which an FDM printer handles. A printed thumbscrew in a printed thread then replaces both the metal screw and the heat-set insert, and paper drag of about 0.5 N is a small load to ask of it.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p7' data-view='p7' data-title='7 · Three ways to grab the arm'></div><h2>Attaching it without touching the arm</h2><p>With a clamp that works, the rest is how the holder grabs the arm. I went through three answers before the one I kept.</p><p>Design 3 was one clamp body with two V clamps 80 mm apart, bolted to the finger with two M2 bolts. It reached paper at base level. But the holes in the finger mesh are only 1.5 × 1.8 mm, too small for M2, so it needed drilling, and I’d ruled out modifying the arm. Those hole sizes come from the mesh; I didn’t measure the real part.</p><p>For design 4, I built the clamps into a replacement fixed jaw, starting from the official printable model. It has three printed parts and an 80 mm clamp span, reaches paper at base level, and passed every design check. It weighs 74 g, though, and fitting it means taking the gripper fully apart and swapping its largest print.</p><p>A replacement moving jaw is tempting for a different reason: the gripper motor could lift the pen off the paper. The catch is slack. Suppose the jaw can turn through 0.5° before the motor resists it. That slack is backlash, and 0.5° is an assumption here; I haven’t measured it. The tip sits 99 mm from the gripper motor axis, so that slack allows about 0.86 mm of movement, already most of the 1.2 mm we want to measure. Keeping the jaw pressed against a stop could prevent it. The design stays a concept, checked for collisions and mass only.</p><p>Design 5 is a sleeve that slides over the stock fixed finger, plus three identical printed M8 thumbscrews. That’s four printed parts and 34 g (a 27.2 g sleeve and 2.1 g per screw), for round pens from 8 to 13 mm. It installs on the stock finger without purchased hardware, disassembly or drilling.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/design5-on-arm.svg' alt='Design 5 sleeve on the SO-101 fixed finger, with the pen vertical in the drawing pose' loading='lazy'><figcaption>Design 5 on the arm in the drawing pose.</figcaption></figure><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='p8' data-view='p8' data-title='8 · How the sleeve stays on'></div><h2>How a sleeve holds on without bolts</h2><p>Without bolts, each direction of load needs its own answer. Upward is the easy one. The sleeve goes on by sliding upward onto the tapered finger end, so when the paper pushes the pen up, it wedges the seat tighter. Sideways, the pocket has 0.15 mm of clearance around the finger, and the side screw presses the finger against the opposite wall to close it. Downward is the weak direction. Only friction at the two contact surfaces holds the sleeve there, with no positive lock. My estimate is 30–60 N of grip against about 0.45 N of weight, and nobody has measured it yet.</p><p>The pen sits tilted 20° in the sleeve, so it hangs vertical in the drawing pose. The two clamps are 43 mm apart because the stock finger is short; on the jaw designs they were 80 mm apart. There’s no sliding clearance anywhere, but the shorter span makes the pen less stiff against tilting, and I haven’t calculated how much the pen bends.</p><details><summary>How each design was built and checked</summary><p>A Python script builds each design with <code>build123d</code> and <code>manifold3d</code> from the stock meshes and a set of parameters. The script checks whole surfaces, so it catches a pen passing through a flat face. Can the moving jaw open fully without hitting the holder? Can the sleeve slide into place? Do six pen sizes from 8 to 13 mm fit? It also checks the fixed jaw and motor, and follows each screw as it turns into its thread to catch collisions with the holder or gripper.</p><p>A wall-thickness scan caught one weak spot. Extra V cavity near the clamp base left two regions of 0.7 mm ring wall along the screw load path. Narrowing the cavity to the bore width brought the ring wall to 3 mm throughout. The thinnest wall left in the sleeve is a 0.85 mm skin between the finger and the pen, which carries almost no load.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-pen-holder/design-history.svg' alt='The The play, spread, retention and tip numbers in this post are CAD and model values. Here is how each one gets measured:</p><ol><li><strong>Play.</strong> Push the tip sideways with an alternating 51 g load and read a dial indicator. Target: under 0.1 mm.</li><li><strong>Reinsertion.</strong> Take the pen out and put it back 10 times, drawing a dot each time, and scan the dots. Target: spread under 0.2 mm.</li><li><strong>Retention.</strong> Hang 500 g (about 5 N) from the sleeve.
