Reinforcement learning is how language models got good at math and code. The recipe fits in a sentence: give the model a prompt, sample 64 answers, score them, and push up the probability of the answers that beat the group average. That's GRPO, and its older sibling PPO trained the first chat models. Robot policies today are built from the same parts (a pretrained vision-language transformer with an action head on top, a VLA, for vision-language-action model), so the obvious move is to run the same RL on a robot. Take a Unitree G1 humanoid with 29 joints, folding a shirt at 50 Hz. What goes wrong?

Two things, it turns out: one in the math and one in the physical world. Let's build up to both, and to the fixes of 2025 and 2026, from the product rule.

## Robots trigger the whole deadly triad

In [Breaking Bellman](/robotics/breaking-bellman/) and the [deadly triad](/robotics/rl-5-the-deadly-triad/) part of my RL series, I looked at three ingredients that make value learning blow up when they meet: function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy data. A toy problem can avoid one of them. A robot needs all three.

- **Function approximation.** A humanoid like Figure 02 or the G1 has 23 to 43 continuous degrees of freedom (DoF, the joints you can command), plus camera pixels. No table can list those states, so everything lives inside neural networks.
- **Bootstrapping.** At 50 Hz, folding a shirt or busing a table is thousands of frames long. Waiting for the end of the episode to hand out credit gives estimates with huge variance, so you bootstrap. You score a step by its reward plus the critic's guess about the next state, $r + \gamma V(s')$, where $r$ is the reward, $\gamma$ is a discount just below 1, and $V(s')$ is the predicted return from the next state $s'$.
- **Off-policy replay.** Robot hours are expensive: motors heat up, and a person has to reset the objects. You can't afford to use a rollout for one gradient step and throw it away. Modern robot RL is the art of off-policy sample efficiency: squeezing the most signal out of every trajectory in the replay buffer, whether it's a demo, an autonomous success, a clumsy near-miss or a failure.

## Why not copy LLM RL?

What about simulation? Train a G1 to walk in Isaac Gym with 4,096 parallel environments and rollouts are cheap, and PPO works great. The catch is the policy. A walking policy is a simple unimodal Gaussian MLP (a small multi-layer perceptron that outputs a mean and a spread for each joint), so $\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$, the log-probability of action $a$ in state $s$ under a policy with weights $\theta$, is known in closed form.

Move to dexterous manipulation with a VLA like $\pi_0$ and you hit two walls:

1. **The math wall, even in simulation.** Manipulation needs multimodal policies, so $\pi_0$ generates actions with flow matching (a cousin of diffusion). For a flow policy, $\log \pi_\theta(a_1 \mid s)$ of the action $a_1$ it outputs has no closed form, so PPO and GRPO can't even compute their loss.
2. **The reset and replay wall, in the real world.** An LLM can clone a prompt 64 times in GPU memory and run critic-free GRPO from identical starting states. A robot can't reset a crumpled shirt to the exact same wrinkles 64 times. And once the robot is deployed, it has to learn off-policy from a replay buffer.

I contribute to Hugging Face's [LeRobot](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot), and that's where I noticed the gap. Open source has the imitation learners ($\pi_0$, ACT) and 2018-era SAC. None of the 2025 to 2026 off-policy generative RL stack is implemented yet.

## 1. Score-function versus pathwise gradients

### The cliff paradox

Let's shrink the robot to one joint so we can draw everything. The action is a number $a$, say a wrist torque. A critic $Q_\phi(s, a)$, a network with weights $\phi$, predicts the return you get after taking action $a$ in state $s$. On the board the critic's curve peaks at $a = +1.5$ with height 8, then drops off a cliff just past $a = +2.0$. The policy is a Gaussian bell centered at $\mu_\theta = 0$, where the critic reads 2, and that is our baseline, $V(s) = 2$.

Now sample one action, and suppose it lands at $a = +2.0$. The critic says $Q_\phi(s, 2.0) = 7$, so the advantage (how much better the action is than the baseline) is $A = 7 - 2 = +5$. But at $a = 2.0$ the curve already slopes down, $\partial Q_\phi / \partial a < 0$, because the peak is behind us. Which way should the bell move?

The two families give opposite answers. PPO reads the +5, concludes that +2.0 was good, and slides the bell right, toward the cliff. SAC reads the slope and slides the bell left, toward the real peak at +1.5. Both are unbiased estimates of the same gradient, yet one sample sends them opposite ways, because they measure different things.

### Family 1: score-function gradients (REINFORCE, PPO, GRPO)

Write the expected return as a weighted sum over the actions the policy might pick:

$$J(\theta) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, Q(s, a)$$

Here $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ is how often the policy picks $a$, and $Q(s, a)$ is the return that $a$ earns. Differentiate with the product rule, $(u \cdot v)' = u'v + uv'$:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_a \Big[\big(\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big)\, Q(s, a) + \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \underbrace{\nabla_\theta Q(s, a)}_{=\,0}\Big]$$

Why is $\nabla_\theta Q(s, a)$ zero? Each $a$ in the sum, like $a = +2.0$, is a fixed point on the axis. Changing the policy weights changes how often the robot picks +2.0. It doesn't change how much reward +2.0 earns in the physical world.

We can't sum over every continuous action, so multiply and divide by $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ and use $\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta / \pi_\theta = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$. The probability then factors out in front, and the sum becomes an expectation we can estimate by sampling:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, Q(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[A(s, a)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\Big]$$

Swapping $Q$ for the advantage $A(s, a) = Q(s, a) - V(s)$ subtracts a baseline, which leaves the expectation unchanged (because $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta] = 0$) and cuts the variance. This is the log-derivative trick, and it is family 1: REINFORCE (1992), PPO (2017) and GRPO (2024). Read it as supervised learning where the label is the action you sampled and the advantage scales the gradient. For a Gaussian with mean $\mu$ and spread $\sigma$, $\partial \log \pi / \partial \mu = (a - \mu)/\sigma^2$, which is positive at $a = 2.0$, and $A = +5$ is positive too, so the mean moves right. The estimator never looks at the critic's slope.

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-clip" data-panel="clip" data-view="clip" data-title="PPO's clip on a stale buffer"></div>

### Why PPO wins in simulation and flatlines on an old buffer

People love PPO when rollouts are free, for a good reason. Its critic $V_\phi(s)$ trains only on fresh rollouts that just happened, and the actor never climbs $\nabla_a Q_\phi$. A critic can be badly wrong about actions nobody tried (call those spikes critic mirages), and PPO never asks it about them, so it is immune to mirages.

Now feed PPO a replay buffer from 3 days ago. PPO replaces $\log \pi_\theta$ with the probability ratio

$$r(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}$$

where $\pi_{\text{old}}$ is the policy that collected the sample, and it clips $r$ to $[0.8, 1.2]$. The objective for one sample is $\min\big(r A,\ \text{clip}(r, 0.8, 1.2)\, A\big)$, plotted on the board. For a good action ($A > 0$) the line climbs until $r = 1.2$ and then goes flat; for a bad action ($A < 0$) it is flat below $r = 0.8$. On those plateaus the gradient is identically zero. Three days of training moved $\pi_\theta$ far from $\pi_{\text{old}}$, so old samples have ratios far outside $[0.8, 1.2]$, and every one that lands on a plateau teaches the policy nothing.

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-pathwise" data-panel="pathwise" data-view="pathwise" data-title="Climbing the critic"></div>

### Where Q-learning sits, and family 2: pathwise gradients (DDPG, TD3, SAC)

Where does the famous Q-learning (DQN) fit? In discrete Q-learning there is no actor network at all. You check all $K$ buttons and pick $a^* = \arg\max_a Q_\phi(s, a)$. On a G1 with 29 joints, you can't loop over $\infty^{29}$ actions to find the max, so you train a network to find it for you. Pathwise RL is continuous Q-learning: the actor $a_\theta(s)$ is an amortized argmax finder, trained to climb $\nabla_a Q_\phi(s, a)$.

A policy still needs a dice roll, for two reasons. Exploration jiggles the torques around the mean to discover better rewards nearby, and multimodality lets one state branch into different valid futures. But you can't backpropagate through $a \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta, \sigma_\theta^2)$, because a random draw isn't a differentiable function of $\theta$. The fix is one line, the reparameterization trick that also trains VAEs. Move the dice roll outside into fixed noise $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ that has no parameters, and write the action as a deterministic formula:

$$a_\theta(s, \epsilon) = \mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta(s)\, \epsilon$$

Now the objective is an expectation over $\epsilon$, and the product rule goes straight through:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon \sim p(\epsilon)}\Big[Q_\phi\big(s, a_\theta(s, \epsilon)\big)\Big] \implies \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\epsilon \sim p(\epsilon)}\Big[\underbrace{\frac{\partial Q_\phi(s, a)}{\partial a}\Big|_{a = a_\theta}}_{1 \times 29} \cdot \underbrace{\nabla_\theta a_\theta(s, \epsilon)}_{29 \times N_{\text{weights}}}\Big]$$

The other product-rule term, $\nabla_\theta p(\epsilon)$, vanishes at once, because $p(\epsilon) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ has no $\theta$ in it. The top of the panel shows the shapes: each of the 29 joints gets its own sign and slope from the critic, and the actor Jacobian turns them into one $1 \times N_{\text{weights}}$ gradient. This is the bell sliding left in the cliff picture.

The price is the loop at the bottom of the panel, the deadly triad in one drawing, which makes training an off-policy actor and critic together unstable:

1. The critic $Q_\phi(s, a)$ grows a random spike at an action the data never covered: a critic mirage.
2. The actor climbs $\nabla_a Q_\phi$ straight onto the mirage.
3. The Bellman backup $y = r + \gamma Q_\phi\big(s', a_\theta(s')\big)$ uses the hallucinated action at the next state $s'$ to update earlier states, and the mirage sucks the surrounding states in like a black hole.

Each classic algorithm adds a band-aid. DDPG (2015) is the bare loop. TD3 (2018) trains twin critics and backs up $\min(Q_1, Q_2)$, so a spike in one critic gets vetoed by the other, and it updates the actor less often than the critics. SAC (2018) keeps a stochastic Gaussian and adds an entropy bonus $-\alpha \log \pi_\theta$, with $\alpha$ a weight, so that $\sigma_\theta$ doesn't collapse to 0 at the peak, where $\nabla_a Q_\phi = 0$ and nothing else keeps the policy exploring.

### What each family needs from the policy

The two families side by side:

| | Family 1: score-function | Family 2: pathwise |
|---|---|---|
| Gradient | $A \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$ | $\nabla_a Q_\phi \cdot \nabla_\theta a_\theta$ |
| Needs | an exact $\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ | a short differentiable path from $\theta$ to $a$ |
| Data | fresh, on-policy | any replay buffer |
| Fails by | zero gradient on stale data | climbing critic mirages |

A robot wants the off-policy column without the mirages. Keep the "Needs" row in mind, because section 3 takes away both entries.

## 2. Multimodality versus tractable likelihoods

### The mug in the way

Back to the whole robot. The G1 has to reach past a coffee mug. Half the demonstrations go left of it ($a = -1$) and half go right ($a = +1$), and both are fine. A unimodal Gaussian MLP trained on them puts its mean at the average, $a = 0$, straight into the mug (I worked through this failure in [mode averaging](/robotics/rl-8-mode-averaging/)). So manipulation needs a policy that can hold two answers at once, and that's where LLM RL starts to look like a square peg in a round hole.

An LLM has a big advantage here that's easy to miss. Its output is a discrete softmax, say over 1,024 token bins, and the probabilities must sum to one, $\sum_{k=1}^{1024} \pi_\theta(k \mid s) = 1$. So every cross-entropy update is a two-for-one. Pushing up the logit of the correct token automatically pushes down the probabilities of all 1,023 wrong tokens through the denominator. You get that built-in contrast, plus multimodality (two good bins can both hold mass), plus an exact $\log \pi_\theta$ in a single pass. That's why Physical Intelligence built FAST, which compresses continuous action chunks with a JPEG-style discrete cosine transform and turns them into discrete tokens.

Continuous flow matching has no such contrast. Pulling a velocity arrow toward a good action at one point does nothing to push down wrong actions anywhere else.

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-flow" data-panel="flow" data-view="flow" data-title="Flow matching in 10 steps"></div>

### Flow matching: iterative test-time thinking

Here is what $\pi_0$ does. Its output is an action chunk: the next 50 control steps for all 29 joints, $50 \times 29 = 1{,}450$ continuous numbers (recomputed as the robot moves, with overlapping chunks merged). Generating all 1,450 numbers in a single network pass forces one forward pass to do all the work. Diffusion and flow matching bring iterative test-time compute to continuous space. The same network is called $K = 10$ times in a loop, and each call refines the chunk a little, from pure noise $a_0$ to a clean action chunk $a_1$.

Training is self-supervised on the replay buffer. Take any recorded chunk $a_1$, pair it with a fresh noise draw $a_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, pick a random time $t \in [0, 1]$, and put a point on the straight line between them, $a_t = (1 - t)\, a_0 + t\, a_1$. The network $v_\theta(a_t, t, s)$ learns, with a squared error, to predict the whole arrow $a_1 - a_0$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t, a_0, a_1}\Big[\big\| v_\theta(a_t, t, s) - (a_1 - a_0) \big\|^2\Big]$$

That's conditional flow matching (CFM). The training step and the sampler fit in a few lines:

```python
# train: one step on a batch of recorded chunks
a1 = buffer.sample_chunks()          # (B, 50, 29)
a0 = randn_like(a1)                  # noise
t  = rand(B, 1, 1)                   # a time in [0, 1] per sample
at = (1 - t) * a0 + t * a1           # a point on the straight line
loss = ((v(at, t, s) - (a1 - a0)) ** 2).mean()

# act: 10 Euler steps from noise to a chunk
a = randn(50, 29)
for k in range(10):
    a = a + 0.1 * v(a, k / 10, s)
```

One thing confused me at first. Why is the target still the full arrow $a_1 - a_0$ at $t = 0.9$, when the point has almost arrived? Because $v_\theta$ is a speedometer (mph), and the distance left to go is a different quantity (miles). Drive a 60-mile straight road from $t = 0$ to $t = 1$ hour at constant speed. The speedometer reads 60 mph at $t = 0$, at $t = 0.5$, and still at $t = 0.9$, when you're at mile 54. You don't overshoot mile 60, because the last step only lasts $\Delta t = 0.1$ hour: $54 + 0.1 \times 60 = 60$. A constant-velocity target also keeps the target size the same for every $t$, so the gradients never vanish near $t = 1$.

If the training paths are straight, why take 10 steps at test time? Look at the panel. Straight lines run from a Gaussian cloud at $t = 0$ to two targets, $a_1 = +1$ and $a_1 = -1$, at $t = 1$. Near $t = 0$ the lines cross each other, so at any point there the network has seen arrows going both ways, and $v_\theta(a_0, 0, s)$ predicts their average. One big step would put every sample on the average, which is the mug. Steps of $0.1\, v_\theta$ let each particle re-check its direction while the left and right streams un-cross and split into two clean branches.

## 3. Why flow matching breaks both standard RL families

### Family 1 breaks: squeezed volume and the Jacobian trace

PPO and GRPO need $\log \pi_\theta(a_1 \mid s)$. So what is the probability density of the chunk a flow produces? Follow a slice of the noise of width $\Delta a_0 = 1.0$ that holds 30% of the probability mass. The arrows $v_\theta$ converge as they carry it, and at $t = 1$ the same 30% sits in a slice of width $\Delta a_1 = 0.01$. Density is mass over width, so it went from $0.3 / 1.0 = 0.3$ to $0.3 / 0.01 = 30$, a 100× jump. To know the density at the end, you have to track how much the flow squeezed the volume along the way.

The sampler is an ODE (an ordinary differential equation, here $da_t/dt = v_\theta(a_t, t, s)$), and the rate at which it squeezes volume is the divergence of $v_\theta$: the sum of the diagonal entries (the trace) of the $29 \times 29$ Jacobian $\partial v_\theta / \partial a_t$, the matrix of how each joint's velocity changes with each joint's position. Integrate it along the path:

$$\log \pi_\theta(a_1 \mid s) = \log p_0(a_0) - \int_0^1 \left(\sum_{i=1}^{29} \frac{\partial v_{\theta,i}(a_t, t, s)}{\partial a_{t,i}}\right) dt$$

Here $p_0$ is the Gaussian noise density and $v_{\theta,i}$ is the velocity of joint $i$. For the 1-D slice, the integral is $\ln(\Delta a_1 / \Delta a_0) = \ln 0.01 \approx -4.6$, and subtracting it adds 4.6 to the log density, a factor of 100. With 29 joints, each diagonal entry costs a backward pass, so that's 29 backward passes at every one of the 10 ODE steps, and PPO then wants to differentiate all of it again with respect to $\theta$. At VLA scale it's intractable.

<details>
<summary>What if we constrain the Jacobian?</summary>

Researchers have tried constraining flow layers so that the Jacobian is triangular or the flow is reversible: SAC-NF ([arXiv:1905.06893](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06893)), NFPO ([arXiv:2603.11470](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.11470)), PrT-Flow ([arXiv:2610.17667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17667)) and GenPO++ ([arXiv:2606.06967](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06967)). Others bound it with spectral normalization so the flow is Lipschitz ([arXiv:2504.08246](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08246)). The catch is that triangular or bi-Lipschitz Jacobians restrict what each layer can express ([arXiv:2107.07232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07232)), and they rule out the standard unconstrained transformer action experts like the one in $\pi_0$.

</details>

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-chain" data-panel="chain" data-view="chain" data-title="Ten Jacobians multiply"></div>

### Family 2 breaks: ten Jacobians multiply into a mirage

Fine, skip the likelihood and climb the critic like SAC. The sampler is differentiable, so backpropagate $\nabla_{a_1} Q_\phi$ through it. Write out the first two Euler steps:

$$a_{0.1} = a_0 + 0.1\, v_\theta(a_0, 0, s), \qquad a_{0.2} = a_{0.1} + 0.1\, v_\theta(a_{0.1}, 0.1, s)$$

The output of step 1, $a_{0.1}$, goes inside $v_\theta$ as the input of step 2. So the 10 steps are nested functions, $f_{10}(f_9(\dots f_1(a_0)))$, and the chain rule multiplies their Jacobians instead of adding them. Following $\theta$'s effect on the first step all the way to the critic:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \frac{\partial Q_\phi(s, a_1)}{\partial a_1} \cdot \left[\prod_{k=1}^{9}\left(I + 0.1\, \frac{\partial v_\theta(a_{t_k})}{\partial a_{t_k}}\right)\right] \cdot \frac{\partial a_{0.1}}{\partial \theta}$$

Here $t_k = k/10$ and $I$ is the identity matrix, and the other nine steps contribute terms of the same shape. That is backpropagation through a 10-layer RNN. If one factor stretches a direction by 1.3, nine of them stretch it by $1.3^9 \approx 10.6$; a factor of 0.7 shrinks it to $0.7^9 \approx 0.04$. And the signal at the end of the chain is the critic's slope in a 1,450-D space where the data covers only a thin manifold. The amplified gradient steers the chunk off that manifold into a critic mirage: the black hole from section 1, with ten amplifiers in front of it.

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-patches" data-panel="patches" data-view="patches" data-title="Two PPO patches that failed"></div>

## 4. Family 3: turn RL into prompted behavior cloning

### Patching PPO onto flows: πRL and FPO

The first camp kept PPO and patched the flow.

Patch 1 is $\pi$RL (and DPPO before it). Deterministic Euler steps have $\sigma = 0$, and a Gaussian $\log \pi_\theta$ divides by $\sigma$. So $\pi$RL injects Gaussian noise into all 10 denoising steps and treats each one as a step of a Markov decision process (MDP), with its own exact Gaussian log-probability. The cost is the horizon. A 50-step episode becomes $50 \times 10 = 500$ steps, and credit assignment over 500 steps is much harder.

Patch 2 is FPO. It uses the change in flow-matching loss as a stand-in for the change in $\log \pi_\theta$ (the loss gives an ELBO, an evidence lower bound on the log-likelihood) and plugs it into PPO's $[0.8, 1.2]$ clip. Physical Intelligence tested FPO and PPO on real-robot laundry folding and reported what happened in [Appendix D of the $\pi^*_{0.6}$ paper (arXiv:2511.14759)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.14759). The noisy MSE proxy lied about how far the policy had moved. It failed to trigger the clip, wrecked the pretrained VLA weights, and training collapsed unless the learning rate was turned down so low that the robot barely improved. On the board, the proxy's needle stays inside the guardrail while the real ratio has left it.

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-cfg" data-panel="cfg" data-view="cfg" data-title="RECAP and classifier-free guidance"></div>

### RECAP ($\pi^*_{0.6}$): the reward becomes a prompt

The second camp noticed something. Supervised behavior cloning (BC) on a flow, $\|v_\theta - (a_1 - a_0)\|^2$, never needed $\log \pi_\theta$ or $\nabla_a Q_\phi$. So turn the reward into an input. RECAP trains a state critic $V_\phi(s)$ offline with 201 value bins. It scores only states in the buffer, so it's safe from mirages. That critic labels every trajectory in the buffer with a prompt token, $I = \text{Good}$ for the top 30% by advantage and $I = \text{Bad}$ for the rest, and RECAP runs plain, unweighted BC with the token as an extra input:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RECAP}}(\theta) = \big\| v_\theta(a_t, t, s, I) - (a_1 - a_0) \big\|^2$$

Why train on the bad trajectories at all? Two reasons.

The first is representation. Advantage-weighted regression (AWR) multiplies the squared error by a weight $w = e^{A/\beta}$, with $\beta$ a temperature. The weight must stay positive, because a negative weight on a squared error rewards the network for making the error huge and sends $\|v_\theta\| \to \infty$. In practice AWR throws away 80 to 90% of the buffer. RECAP trains on 100% of the trajectories with weight 1.0, which forces the VLA's representations to separate what makes a motion succeed from what makes it fail, across every physical state in the data.

The second is classifier-free guidance (CFG), and the name says what it skips. Instead of differentiating a separate classifier or critic at test time, you query the flow network twice: once with no label ($v_{\text{all}}$, the average over Good and Bad) and once with $I = \text{Good}$ ($v_{\text{good}}$). If you know Anthropic's work on sparse autoencoders and activation steering, where subtracting a "sycophancy" direction from an LLM's activations steers its behavior, this will look familiar. The difference $v_{\text{good}} - v_{\text{all}}$ is a concept vector in velocity space. It points from the mixed average through Good, and therefore directly away from Bad:

$$v_{\text{guided}} = v_{\text{all}} + \beta\, \big(v_{\text{good}} - v_{\text{all}}\big)$$

Here $\beta$ is the guidance weight (a different $\beta$ from AWR's temperature). Put numbers on it, as on the board. Good is 30% of the data and pulls with $v_{\text{good}} = (0.7, 1)$, and Bad pulls with $v_{\text{bad}} = (-0.3, 1)$, so $v_{\text{all}} = 0.3\,(0.7, 1) + 0.7\,(-0.3, 1) = (0, 1)$. With $\beta = 2$, $v_{\text{guided}} = (0, 1) + 2\,(0.7, 0) = (1.4, 1)$. The policy extrapolates past $v_{\text{good}}$ and actively repels the failure modes.

```python
# label once, with the 201-bin state critic V
for traj in buffer:
    traj.I = "Good" if advantage(V, traj) in top_30_percent else "Bad"

# train: plain flow-matching BC on 100% of the data, the token as input
# (dropping I on some steps teaches the unlabeled v_all)
loss = ((v(at, t, s, I) - (a1 - a0)) ** 2).mean()

# act: two network calls per Euler step
for k in range(10):
    v_all  = v(a, k / 10, s, I=None)
    v_good = v(a, k / 10, s, I="Good")
    a = a + 0.1 * (v_all + 2.0 * (v_good - v_all))
```

The simulator below is a toy version. Seen from above, each action is a 2D point where the gripper passes the mug. Clean passes on the left and the right are the top 30% of the demonstrations, labeled Good, and a crash through the mug and two clumsy brushes past it are Bad. The velocity field is the exact flow-matching solution for this data, which is what a perfect network would learn. Set one Euler step and every sample lands on the average, inside the mug. Then raise the steps, and move $\beta$ from 0 (all data) through 1 (Good only) to 2. At 2 steps, $\beta = 1$ still leaves 34% of the samples nearest a Bad cluster, and $\beta = 2$ cuts that to 18%.

### Richer prompts ($\pi_{0.7}$) and actions in the output (GVC)

$\pi_{0.7}$ ([arXiv:2604.15483](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15483)) replaces the 1-bit tag $I \in \{0, 1\}$ of $\pi^*_{0.6}$ with three richer metadata prompts, `Speed`, `Quality` (1 to 5) and `Mistake` (true or false), plus a visual subgoal image 4 s ahead. That distills all the task specialists into one steerable generalist.

The Generative Value Classifier, GVC ([Swerdlow, Dong and Finn, arXiv:2610.22497](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.22497)), pushes the same flip into the critic. It starts from a rule of deep learning that's easy to forget: inputs create no supervision, and only the outputs in the loss do. A standard critic $Q_\phi(s, a) \to \hat{R}$ puts the 1,450-D action chunk in the input and supervises one scalar in the output, $(\hat{R} - R)^2$, where $\hat{R}$ is the predicted return and $R$ is the observed one. One number per sample starves the critic of signal.

GVC moves the return into the input, as one of $K$ discrete return bins $z_1, \dots, z_K$, and puts the 1,450-D action velocity in the output target, $\|v_\theta(a_t, t, s, z_i) - (a_1 - a_0)\|^2$. Every sample now supervises 1,450 numbers instead of 1, which is 1,450× more supervision.

The use case is Best-of-$N$ at test time. The policy proposes $N = 16$ candidate chunks. For each candidate $a$, GVC checks which return bin $z_i$ gives the smallest flow-matching error on $a$: a small error means that bin explains the chunk well. Bayes' rule turns the errors into probabilities over the bins, with a sum over all $K$ bins in the denominator,

$$p(z_i \mid s, a) = \frac{p(a \mid s, z_i)\, p(z_i \mid s)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} p(a \mid s, z_j)\, p(z_j \mid s)}$$

and the expected return is $Q_{\text{GVC}}(s, a) = \sum_i p(z_i \mid s, a)\, z_i$. A TD value anchor, the loss $(Q_{\text{GVC}} - y_{\text{TD}})^2$ with $y_{\text{TD}}$ the bootstrapped target $r + \gamma V(s')$, keeps the numbers calibrated. The robot executes the best of the 16.

```python
cands = [policy.sample(s) for _ in range(16)]

def q_gvc(a):
    err = array([flow_error(a, s, z) for z in bins])  # small error: z explains a
    p = softmax(-err + log_prior(s))                  # Bayes over the K bins
    return (p * bins).sum()                           # expected return

execute(max(cands, key=q_gvc))
```

## 5. Beyond cloning the dataset: family 4

### Breaking the behavior-cloning ceiling

Family 3 has a ceiling. Prompt-conditioned BC like RECAP and $\pi_{0.7}$ can only interpolate between trajectories that already exist in the replay buffer. How do we find actions better than anything in the buffer without backpropagating through 10 ODE steps? The frontier has two answers.

**4A: freeze the VLA and steer it.** Policy Decorator ([Yuan et al., arXiv:2412.13630](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.13630)), PLD ([arXiv:2511.00091](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00091)) and MiDAS ([arXiv:2608.11363](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11363)) freeze the pretrained VLA and train a tiny residual edit $\Delta a$ on top of its action. DSRL ([Wagenmaker, Levine et al., arXiv:2506.15799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15799)) and FRS ([arXiv:2606.13675](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13675)) freeze the flow VLA $\theta$ completely and train a lightweight RL policy to pick which starting noise vector $a_0$ goes into the ODE. Every $a_0$ in the Gaussian ball decodes into a realistic motion, so critic mirages in action space become impossible.

**4B: tilt the velocity with a one-step preview.** QF3 ([Kim, McAllister, Goldberg, Abbeel, Kanazawa, arXiv:2610.08789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08789)) and FlowDPG ([arXiv:2606.22303](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22303)), in the line of Q-score matching, never backpropagate $\nabla_a Q_\phi$ through all 10 steps. At a single step they take a preview of where the flow is heading, $\hat{a}_1 = a_t + (1 - t)\, v_\theta$ (the speedometer again: speed times the time left), ask the critic which direction improves $\hat{a}_1$, and tilt the velocity arrow that way. The gradient passes through one step, so the product of ten Jacobians never forms.

### Fixing $\pi_{0.7}$'s pixel subgoals, coarse tags and amnesiac states

$\pi_{0.7}$ leaves three things to fix.

Its subgoals are pixels. A 14B image model takes 1.25 s on 4× H100 to render an RGB picture of the future, and the policy then re-encodes the picture into ViT tokens (the patch embeddings of a vision transformer). That's a temporary stepping stone. Predicting subgoals directly in the latent ViT embedding space is 100× faster, and it keeps hindsight experience replay (HER), which relabels a trajectory with the goal it actually reached. If the arm smoothly grabs the wrong corner of the shirt, the subgoal gets `Mistake: true`, and the low-level flow controller still gets `Quality: 5` for reaching that latent state.

Its tags are coarse: `Quality` and `Speed` label whole episodes or coarse segments, with no dense credit for each step.

Its state has no memory. Current VLAs see only the current frame $o_t$, or 2 frames. If a heavy box slips out of the grasp at $t = 1$ s, the camera frame at $t = 2$ s looks identical, and the policy repeats the failed grasp forever. Add a compressed episodic memory-imprint vector to the state, encoding the actions it recently tried and the critic's surprise $\Delta V$ (how far the predicted value jumped), and the policy can adapt to the physics in context at test time.

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-imagine" data-panel="imagine" data-view="imagine" data-title="Imagine, score, act"></div>

### The finale: simulate before you act

Max Bennett's *A Brief History of Intelligence* describes one of the defining leaps in vertebrate brain evolution: the ability to pause and simulate candidate actions inside an internal world model before acting. A cat or a squirrel on a branch pauses, mentally tries three different jumps, scores whether each one lands safely or falls, and only then executes the winner. LLMs do something like it with token-level chain of thought. Physical robots have lacked fast internal physical simulation with action scoring.

The pieces of this article converge on that loop, family 4C, which is AlphaZero's loop for physical robots:

1. **Propose.** Given the state and its memory imprint, the multimodal flow policy proposes $N = 16$ diverse candidate chunks: a left grasp, a right grasp, a fast push, a gentle hook.
2. **Simulate.** A fast latent world model rolls the 16 chunks forward in embedding space in milliseconds, and GVC scores the 16 outcomes in parallel.
3. **Act and distill.** The robot executes the best of the 16 and logs the winning trajectory back into the replay buffer as a new `Quality: 5` experience, so the base flow reflex absorbs it on the next training pass.

```python
while True:
    s = observe() + memory_imprint()
    cands  = [flow_policy.sample(s) for _ in range(16)]       # propose
    future = [world_model.rollout(s, a) for a in cands]       # simulate, in latent space
    best   = max(range(16), key=lambda i: gvc(future[i], cands[i]))
    execute(cands[best])                                      # act
    buffer.add(s, cands[best], quality=5)                     # distill on the next pass
```

The squirrel on the branch is a decent spec for a household robot. In open source we're still at imitation learning and 2018-era SAC, so every box in that loop is open work for LeRobot.
