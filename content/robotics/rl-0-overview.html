Reinforcement learning is how a robot gets better from its own attempts, and how language models get tuned on feedback. Then you open a paper and run into Q-learning, PPO, CQL... okay, why do we need all these things? I think there's a much simpler way to hold them in your head. Nearly every name on that list patches one specific way that learning from reward breaks. Once you can see the breaks, picking a tool turns into reading symptoms.

So let's take one task and watch it break. A robot arm (the agent) looks at a camera image (the observation), sends a joint command (the action) and gets a reward. The rule that maps an image to a command is the policy, and learning means changing that rule so the arm collects more reward per attempt (an episode). Suppose the arm gets 0 for reaching, 0 for going left around a box, 0 for grasping, and finally +4 for lifting the cup. Great! But which command should we make more likely next time? The lift? The grasp? Going left, two commands before the lift? The board follows this one reach from top to bottom.

Every fix on that board answers a limitation in something earlier, and a fix can open a new way to fail. Much of the field grew like this, which is why no row in panel 5 works on every run. So before you pick an algorithm, ask what actually went wrong. Did the arm ever try the other route? Did the +4 ever reach the early commands? Did one update make a useful command much less likely? I think these questions are more useful at the start than another list of algorithm names. Name the mechanism behind the answer, check that your data meets the tool's condition, and only then pick the algorithm.

Most of these methods, though not all, repeat the same two moves: estimate how well the arm's current policy will do, then change the policy to favor the commands that look best under that estimate. That loop is generalized policy iteration. When the estimates break, the policy follows them, which is why the board spends most of its space on how estimates break. Where each symptom gets its full treatment in the series

  • Policy lurches: PPO clipping, shared weights.
  • Values drift off target: bootstrapping, time limits, on-policy and off-policy learning, the deadly triad, shown with 2 states and 1 weight, actions missing from fixed data.
