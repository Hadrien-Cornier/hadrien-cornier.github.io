You win a game after a hundred moves. Which earlier choices deserve credit, and what should you try next? Reinforcement learning answers through rewards and estimates of future reward. We will build those estimates, compare the problems each method solves, then see how a guess can reinforce its own error. The route follows [Sutton and Barto, Chapters 2–7 and 11](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf).

**Codex trial · Part 0.** [Part 1: the Q table](/robotics/rl-1-q-learning-codex/). All six trial episodes are linked at the end.

Throughout, $s$ is a state, $a$ an action, $r$ a reward and $s'$ the next state. A **policy** $\pi$ picks actions; the **discount** $\gamma$ reduces later rewards. $\mathbb{E}$ means average, $\max$ the largest value, and $\arg\max$ the action attaining it. A prime marks the next state or action; a time subscript locates a quantity in the sequence. In equations, $\sum$ adds the indexed terms and $\leftarrow$ replaces the old estimate with the expression on its right.

**Reading goal:** follow one problem at a time: delayed rewards lead to value estimates; imperfect targets lead to different methods; sharing estimates creates the stability question. Use the family tree to pick a branch, then open its worked explanation.

## Credit assignment

You play a whole game and learn at the end whether you won. Which earlier choices led there? Let $t$ count moves, $R_{t+1}$ be the reward after the current move, and $G_t$ the return from then onward. The return adds those later rewards, discounting each by $\gamma$ for every extra step of delay. The dots continue the same pattern until the episode ends, or indefinitely in a discounted continuing task.

$$\begin{aligned} G_t = R_{t+1} &+ \gamma R_{t+2} \\ &+ \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \cdots \end{aligned}$$

A value function estimates the **expected return** from a state or action. Learning those expectations lets earlier choices reflect later rewards.

On the drawing: the six-step strip uses an illustrative reward of +1 and $\gamma=0.9$. Read right to left: each earlier step adds a factor of 0.9.

## Explore or exploit: the bandit

Which of ten slot machines should you pull? Each pays a hidden average plus noise. This **bandit** has one state, so its table has one row: $Q(a)$ estimates machine $a$'s mean payout; $n(a)$ counts its pulls, including the latest one, and $r$ is that pull's reward. Let $\varepsilon$ be the probability of exploring with a uniformly random machine.

$$Q(a) \leftarrow Q(a) + \tfrac{1}{n(a)}\bigl(r - Q(a)\bigr)$$

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Ten slot machines, 1,000 pulls (ε = 0 is greedy, ε = 0.1 explores a little)</div>
<ol>
<li>Set Q(a) = 0 and n(a) = 0 for each machine a.</li>
<li>Repeat for 1,000 pulls:</li>
<li class="i1">With probability ε pick a random machine. Otherwise pick argmax Q(a), at random on a tie.</li>
<li class="i1">Pull it and observe the payout r. Set n(a) = n(a) + 1.</li>
<li class="i1">Q(a) ← Q(a) + (r − Q(a)) / n(a)</li>
</ol>
</div>

The factor $1/n(a)$ makes this a running sample average: each new payout receives one equal share of the updated mean.

For example, a machine with two payouts totaling 4 has estimate 2. A third payout of −1 gives $2+(-1-2)/3=1$, the mean of all three payouts. The table stores that running mean, not the latest reward. [Sutton and Barto, §2.4](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf). Greedy picks the largest estimate and can stay with a lucky early choice. With $\varepsilon=0.1$, one pull in ten explores, so other estimates get a chance to improve.

## Training the Q function

A move can earn zero now and still be the only way to reach a later reward. Updating from the immediate reward alone would miss that choice. With several states, $Q(s,a)$ estimates the return after action $a$ in state $s$. A **target** is the return we train it toward. For tables, the update below moves one estimate a fraction $\alpha$, the step size, toward its target. Keeping the target fixed for that update makes the residual smaller. The next three sections change what supplies that target; n-step TD also works on state values $V(s)$. [Sutton and Barto, §6.5](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf).

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">The tabular action-value update</div>
<ol>
<li>Pick an action a in the state s (greedy, or random with probability ε).</li>
<li>Take it. Observe the reward r and the next state s′.</li>
<li>Build a target for Q(s, a).</li>
<li>Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α · (target − Q(s, a))</li>
</ol>
<div class="algo-note">α is the step size. Compare targets in line 3; the methods also vary timing, behavior and representation.</div>
</div>

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-horizons" data-panel="horizons" data-num="4a" data-view="horizons" data-title="How long before a guess?"></div>

For one illustrative update, the old entry is 2, the target is 6 and $\alpha=0.25$. The new entry is $2+0.25(6-2)=3$. The loop changes one number; the choice of target decides what future that number learns.

### How long before a guess?

How long must we wait before we can train on an outcome? An **episode** ends at a terminal state, such as a win or loss. **[Monte Carlo](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf)** learns from its completed return:

$$\text{target} = G_t$$

**Use:** learn from full outcomes. **Needs:** completed episodes. **Cost:** waiting and noisy returns.

Waiting for the end delays learning. **[Q-learning](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/cjch.pdf)** replaces the rest with the best estimate in the next row:

$$\text{target} = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a')$$

**Use:** learn every move. **Needs:** a Markov state, containing what predicts the next transition. **Cost:** a guessed tail (**bootstrapping**). Target minus old estimate is the **temporal difference (TD) error**.

Let $n$ be the backup length and $k$ index the rewards from 0 to $n-1$. $S_{t+n}$ is the state reached after those $n$ transitions; $V$ estimates its remaining return. A late outcome takes successive one-step updates to travel backward. **[n-step TD](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf)** includes $n$ real rewards before its guess:

$$\text{target} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma^k R_{t+k+1} + \gamma^n V(S_{t+n})$$

Here $V$ estimates state values. **Use:** longer backups. **Needs:** stored transitions. **Cost:** waiting $n$ steps. At an episode's end, shorten the return and omit the terminal guess.

**On the drawing, 4a:** real rewards come first, then a guess. The six-step episode and $n=3$ are illustrative.

<details>
<summary>Algorithms: completed return, one step, n steps</summary>

These are three ways to fill the same target slot. In the backward loop, $G$ is a running return, initialized to zero at termination. The one-step and n-step loops replace only the unobserved tail with an estimate; terminal tails are zero. [Part 2](/robotics/rl-2-bootstrapping-codex/) works through when each update can start.

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Monte Carlo target</div>
<ol>
<li>Finish the episode. Set G = 0 at its end.</li>
<li>Walk backward: G ← reward + γ · G. Use G as the target for that earlier state and action.</li>
</ol>
</div>

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Q-learning target</div>
<ol>
<li>Take a in s. Observe r and s′.</li>
<li>If s′ ends the episode: target = r. Otherwise: target = r + γ · max Q(s′, ·).</li>
</ol>
</div>

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">n-step state-value target</div>
<ol>
<li>Collect n rewards, or stop earlier at the end of the episode.</li>
<li>Discount and sum the collected rewards. If the episode continues, add γⁿ · V(the state reached).</li>
<li>Move V(the starting state) toward this target.</li>
</ol>
</div>

</details>

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-behavior" data-panel="behavior" data-num="4b" data-view="behavior" data-title="Which next action counts?"></div>

### Which next action counts?

Near a cliff, your best move may be safe while a random exploratory move makes you fall. Q-learning's max values the best next choice. To include the cost of your behavior, **[Sarsa](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf)** uses the next action you actually pick:

$$\text{target} = r + \gamma\, Q(s',a')$$

State, Action, Reward, next State, next Action gives the name. **Use:** value exploratory behavior. **Needs:** $a'$ comes from that policy. **Cost:** next-action sampling noise.

Sampling one next action also makes the target fluctuate. When you know its probabilities, **[Expected Sarsa](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf)** averages over that choice:

$$\text{target} = r + \gamma \sum_{a'} \pi(a'\mid s')\,Q(s',a')$$

**Use:** remove next-action sampling noise. **Needs:** known policy probabilities. **Cost:** score every action. On sketch 4b, the same row 2, 5, 3 gives a max of 5, a sample of 3, and a weighted average of 4.5.

<details>
<summary>Algorithms: Sarsa and Expected Sarsa</summary>

Keep the transition and next-state row fixed. Sarsa uses one sampled next action; Expected Sarsa sums over its policy probabilities. Both then use the same target-minus-estimate update from the training loop. [Part 4](/robotics/rl-4-on-policy-and-off-policy-codex/) calculates both targets from one row.

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Sarsa target</div>
<ol>
<li>Observe r and s′. Choose the next action a′ using your current policy.</li>
<li>Use target = r + γ · Q(s′, a′). At the terminal state, use r alone.</li>
</ol>
</div>

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Expected Sarsa target</div>
<ol>
<li>For every next action, multiply its Q value by its probability under π.</li>
<li>Sum those products. Use target = r + γ · sum. At the terminal state, use r alone.</li>
</ol>
</div>

</details>

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-double" data-panel="double" data-num="4c" data-view="double" data-title="Pick and score separately"></div>

### Pick and score separately

When estimates are noisy, picking the largest also tends to pick lucky noise. **[Double Q-learning](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2010/hash/091d584fced301b442654dd8c23b3fc9-Abstract.html)** reduces this bias: one table picks the next action and the other scores it. Write those tables as $Q_1$ and $Q_2$; $\text{target}_1$ is the target when updating the first.

$$\text{target}_1 = r + \gamma\, Q_2\big(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_1(s',a')\big)$$

**Use:** reduce maximization bias. **Needs:** separately learned estimates. **Cost:** two tables sharing the updates. On sketch 4c, all true values are 0; the illustrative pick of 0.9 gets a separate score of −0.1.

<details>
<summary>Algorithm: Double Q-learning</summary>

Keep both tables across steps. When updating $Q_1$, select the action with $Q_1$ and evaluate it with $Q_2$; swap the roles when updating $Q_2$. Apply the usual step-size update to the chosen table only. Separate scoring reduces selection bias; it does not guarantee an unbiased estimate at every finite step. [Van Hasselt, 2010](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2010/hash/091d584fced301b442654dd8c23b3fc9-Abstract.html).

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Double Q-learning target</div>
<ol>
<li>Choose a table to update at random: each receives half the updates on average.</li>
<li>That table picks the best next action. The other table scores it.</li>
<li>Build the target from r plus γ times that score; update only the chosen table. Terminal target = r.</li>
</ol>
</div>

</details>

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-sharing" data-panel="sharing" data-num="4d" data-view="sharing" data-title="Share across states"></div>

### Share across states

A separate row for every camera image would leave almost every row unvisited. **[DQN](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14236)** replaces the table with a network that shares learning across states. Shared weights also make targets move as you learn, so replay reuses transitions and a frozen target network slows those changes.

$\theta$ denotes learned network weights; $\bar\theta$ is their frozen copy. $Q_{\bar\theta}$ evaluates the next action with that copy.

$$\text{target} = r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\bar\theta}(s',a')$$

 **Use:** large state spaces. **Needs:** useful learned features. **Cost:** training and stability problems.

**On sketch 4d:** shared weights can change other predictions. The colored pattern is one illustrative outcome; learned representations determine useful sharing.

<details>
<summary>Algorithm: DQN</summary>

The tuple stores state, action, reward, next state and whether the episode ended. Replay changes which transition is fitted; the copied weights hold its guessed tail steady between refreshes. Fit the online network with the target treated as a constant. [Part 3](/robotics/rl-3-function-approximation-codex/) explains that derivative choice; [part 5](/robotics/rl-5-the-deadly-triad-codex/) explains what freezing does and does not fix.

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">DQN update</div>
<ol>
<li>Save a transition (s, a, r, s′, terminal) in replay memory.</li>
<li>Sample saved transitions. Build targets with the frozen network; terminal target = r.</li>
<li>Adjust θ so Qθ(s, a) moves toward those targets. Periodically copy θ into the target network.</li>
</ol>
</div>

</details>

## The family tree

One tree, eight problems. Follow a numbered branch to see why the next method exists. Blue nodes mark series topics. Adapted from my game [Breaking Bellman](https://hadrien-cornier.github.io/breaking-bellman/), this is a teaching map, not an invention timeline.

Read a branch in three steps: its label names what the parent method cannot do well, the arrow points to a response, and the child method changes that particular mechanism. For example, Monte Carlo waits for the final reward; the arrow toward Q-learning replaces the unfinished tail with a guess. The earlier target sketches show the arithmetic.

The overview keeps every branch together. On a phone, use **+** to read its labels and swipe to move around; **Overview** brings the whole tree back. Tap an edge, or open its explanation below.

## Where the Bellman equation comes from

How can you learn about a whole future from one transition? Here $G_t$ is the return from step $t$, $R_{t+1}$ is its first reward and $G_{t+1}$ is the remaining return. Split after that first reward, then take the expectation. The subscript $\pi$ on an expectation means following policy $\pi$; a vertical bar means conditioning on the stated starting state or action. The Bellman equation links each state's value to the next state's value.

$$G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}$$

On the drawing: factor out $\gamma$, then average over possible futures.

<div class="rc-mark" id="sec-bellman2" data-panel="bellman" data-num="6" data-view="bellman2" data-title="Bellman: average, then max"></div>

<div class="eq-label">Bellman equation for a policy <span>Eq. 3.14</span></div>

$$v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[R_{t+1} + \gamma\, v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s\right]$$

The first equation follows policy $\pi$. For optimal action values, fix the current action $a$, average over the world's resulting transition, then choose the best action after arrival:

<div class="eq-label">Bellman optimality: maximize over the next action <span>Eq. 3.20</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} q_*(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[&R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} q_*(S_{t+1}, a') \\ &\Bigm| S_t = s,\, A_t = a\Bigr] \end{aligned}$$

Once you have $q_*$, taking the action with the largest value in each state is an optimal policy, so solving the second equation solves the problem.

<details>
<summary>Derivation from the return</summary>

The steps below expand, split and average the same reward sequence. $k$ counts rewards ahead of the current time. A fixed policy makes the conditional expected remaining return equal to its value at the next state. This is the bridge from recorded rewards to a reusable prediction.

<div class="eq-label">1. The return <span>Eq. 3.8</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \cdots \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \end{aligned}$$

<div class="eq-label">2. Factor out γ <span>Eq. 3.9</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\bigl(R_{t+2} + \gamma R_{t+3} + \cdots\bigr) \\ &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G_{t+1} \end{aligned}$$

<div class="eq-label">3. The episode ends at step T: nothing is left</div>

$$G_T = 0$$

So the value of a terminal state is 0.

<div class="eq-label">4. Values are expected returns <span>Eq. 3.12 and 3.13</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} v_\pi(s) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s\right] \\ q_\pi(s,a) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s,\, A_t = a\right] \end{aligned}$$

Putting equation 2 into equation 4 gives the Bellman equation for a policy. The value is not a new reward: $R_{t+1}$ is real, and $\gamma\, v_\pi(S_{t+1})$ stands in for everything after it.

</details>

## The deadly triad

Sharing predictions saves memory. Bootstrapping saves waiting. Off-policy learning lets us reuse data. The trouble is that a change to one shared prediction can also change its own future target; off-policy sampling may fail to correct that target often enough. The guesses can reinforce each other instead of settling down. [Van Hasselt et al. show this feedback](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02648#S2).

**The green pairs are specific stable examples, not a guarantee that any two ingredients are safe. All three together can diverge; they do not have to.** The numbered edges have three different reasons for stability.

In the equations below, $t$ is the time step; $S_t$ and $A_t$ are the state and action; $R_{t+1}$ is the next reward. The target policy $\pi(a\mid s)$ gives the probability of action $a$ in state $s$; $b(a\mid s)$ is the policy that collected the data. The discount is $0\leq\gamma<1$ and $\alpha_t>0$ is a learning step size. A superscript $+$ means the next parameter or table value.

<div class="dt-pairs">
<section class="dt-worked" id="dt-pair-1">

### 1 · Approximation + bootstrapping

**Learn a fixed policy's values with on-policy linear TD.** A fixed feature vector $\phi(s)$ describes state $s$; the weights $w$ produce $v_w(s)=\phi(s)^\top w$. The superscript $\top$ means transpose. Let $\delta_t$ be the TD error, and let $w^+$ denote the updated weights. One observed transition gives this update:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta_t &= R_{t+1}+\gamma v_w(S_{t+1})-v_w(S_t),\\
w^+ &= w+\alpha_t\delta_t\phi(S_t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Why can this settle down? For a candidate value vector $v$, let $T^\pi$ be the Bellman prediction operator:

$$
(T^\pi v)(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[
R_{t+1}+\gamma v(S_{t+1})\mid S_t=s].
$$

The expectation uses the fixed target policy and the environment's transition probabilities. Let $D$ put its stationary state probabilities $d(s)$ on the diagonal; $\|x\|_D^2=\sum_s d(s)x(s)^2$ weights a value vector $x$ by those probabilities. Let $\Pi$ be least-squares projection onto the space spanned by the fixed features, using those same weights. For value vectors $v$ and $u$:

$$
\|\Pi T^\pi v-\Pi T^\pi u\|_D
\leq\gamma\|v-u\|_D.
$$

On-policy averaging cannot enlarge this norm; neither can the orthogonal projection. Multiplying by $\gamma<1$ makes the combined map a contraction, with one projected fixed point $v=\Pi T^\pi v$. **TD's sampled weight update is not literally repeated projection.** Under the conditions below, it converges to that same projected solution. [Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, Sections IV and VII](https://web.mit.edu/~jnt/www/Papers/J063-97-bvr-td.pdf).

**Add off-policy sampling:** projection may now weight states by the behavior distribution, while the Bellman target follows $\pi$. The matched-norm argument can fail. The two-state example below shows the resulting feedback.

**Read the bound:** if two candidate value vectors are distance 2 apart and $\gamma=0.9$, one projected Bellman map leaves them at most 1.8 apart. It compares two whole predictions under the same policy and state weighting. It does not say every noisy TD sample improves its estimate.

<details class="dt-conditions">
<summary>Conditions behind this prediction result</summary>
<p>This is a fixed-policy prediction result. A finite irreducible, aperiodic chain supplies a stationary distribution and repeated on-policy visits. Rewards are bounded, the discount is below 1, and the fixed feature columns are linearly independent.</p>
<p>Positive diminishing learning rates have infinite sum and finite squared sum: they retain enough total adjustment while suppressing persistent sampling noise. These assumptions support the linear result cited above; they do not cover arbitrary neural networks or a changing control policy.</p>
</details>
</section>
<section class="dt-worked" id="dt-pair-2">

### 2 · Approximation + off-policy

**Wait for the complete return, then fit fixed targets.** Let $H$ be the terminal time, $k$ index future steps, $G_t$ be the full discounted return and $\rho_t$ be the trajectory weight correcting behavior $b$ to target policy $\pi$. The product $\prod$ multiplies the action-probability ratios along that recorded future. Its return and importance weight are

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_t &= \sum_{k=0}^{H-t-1}\gamma^k R_{t+k+1},\\
\rho_t &= \prod_{k=t}^{H-1}
\frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)}{b(A_k\mid S_k)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Crucially, $G_t$ contains **rewards, not a guessed future value**. Freeze the recorded data. For example $i$, use its corrected return $y_i=\rho_i G_i$ as the label; here $G_i$ and $\rho_i$ mean the return and weight for that recorded starting point. Let $y$ stack these labels, and let row $i$ of the feature matrix $X$ be $\phi(s_i)^\top$, with $s_i$ the example's starting state. Let $\eta>0$ be the batch step size; $X^\top$ is the transposed feature matrix. Batch gradient descent is

$$
w^+=w-\eta X^\top(Xw-y).
$$

The Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ measures vector length. The fixed squared-error loss is $L(w)=\tfrac12\|Xw-y\|_2^2$. Let $w_*$ be its empirical minimizer, $\succ0$ mean positive definite, and $^{-1}$ mean matrix inverse. If $X$ has full column rank,

$$
X^\top X\succ0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
w_*=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.
$$

Full rank gives one empirical least-squares minimizer. Choose $\eta$ below 2 divided by the largest eigenvalue of $X^\top X$: then the error in each curvature direction shrinks. The labels stay fixed while the weights move. Rank-deficient data need not have a unique fit. This is a well-posed regression argument, not a blanket convergence claim for off-policy Monte Carlo. [Sutton and Barto, published second edition, §§5.5 and 9.2](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf).

**Add bootstrapping:** replace the complete return with a target containing $v_w$ at a future state. Now the labels move with $w$. Treating them as constants during each update no longer gives gradient descent on the fixed regression above.

**One fixed label:** take one feature equal to 1 and a corrected return label of 2. The loss is $(w-2)^2/2$. With batch step size $\eta=0.5$, weights $0,1,1.5,1.75$ approach 2 because the label stays 2. Bootstrapping would let that destination move with the predictions.

<details class="dt-conditions">
<summary>Conditions behind this regression example</summary>
<p>Freeze the target and behavior policies, linear features and recorded data. Use independent finite-horizon episodes with bounded rewards and features. Behavior must cover the target actions and the starting states whose values are being fitted; corrected labels need finite variance for statistical claims.</p>
<p>Full column rank gives a unique empirical fit. The batch step-size bound stated above makes optimization approach it. Finite data need not recover the true target-policy value; large importance weights can make that statistical estimate poor.</p>
</details>
</section>
<section class="dt-worked" id="dt-pair-3">

### 3 · Bootstrapping + off-policy

**Keep a separate table entry for every state-action pair.** Let $Q$ be the action-value table. In a transition $(s,a,r,s')$, $s$ and $a$ are the state and action, $r$ the reward and $s'$ the next state; $a'$ ranges over next actions. Tabular Q-learning updates just one entry:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q^+(s,a)=Q(s,a)+\alpha_t\big[&r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\\
&-Q(s,a)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

All other entries stay unchanged. For any candidate table $q$, its expected target is the Bellman optimality operator $T_*$:

$$
(T_*q)(s,a)=\mathbb E\!\left[
 r+\gamma\max_{a'}q(s',a')\mid s,a\right].
$$

The expectation averages the environment's rewards and transitions. The max norm $\|x\|_\infty=\max_{s,a}|x(s,a)|$ is the largest absolute entry of a table $x$. For two candidate tables $q$ and $u$,

$$
\|T_*q-T_*u\|_\infty
\leq\gamma\|q-u\|_\infty.
$$

Taking a maximum and averaging cannot amplify the largest entrywise difference; discounting shrinks it. Thus $T_*$ has one fixed point, the optimal table. With enough visits and suitable step sizes, sampled Q-learning reaches it. [Watkins and Dayan's convergence theorem](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/cjch.pdf).

**Add function approximation:** a weight update can also change other states' values, including a value in the bootstrap target. The tabular Bellman operator still contracts, but that alone does not control the added shared fitting step. Its guarantee does not transfer to an arbitrary approximator.

**Two separate entries:** if the successor maximum is 5, the reward is 0 and $\gamma=0.9$, the target is 4.5. Moving a predecessor from 2 toward 4.5 leaves the successor at 5 during that update. A shared weight can move both; a table entry cannot.

<details class="dt-conditions">
<summary>Conditions behind tabular convergence</summary>
<p>The task is a finite Markov decision process with fixed transition and reward laws, bounded rewards and discount below 1. Every nonterminal state-action pair must receive infinitely many updates.</p>
<p>Each pair's positive learning rates are at most 1, have infinite sum and finite squared sum. A replay buffer omitting a pair fails the visit assumption; a constant noisy step size fails the shrinking-step assumption. The theorem cited above concerns the resulting infinite update sequence, not a finite successful route.</p>
</details>
</section>

### A tiny feedback loop: one weight, two values

Take two states $s_1,s_2$ and one available action. The target policy always takes it: $s_1$ moves to $s_2$, and $s_2$ loops to itself. Every reward is 0 and $\gamma<1$, so both true values are 0. Use one scalar weight $w$, features $\phi(s_1)=1$, $\phi(s_2)=2$, and estimates $v_w(s_1)=w$, $v_w(s_2)=2w$.

Now replay **only** the stored zero-reward transition $s_1\to s_2$, forever. Sampling probability is 1 for that transition and 0 for transitions starting at $s_2$. Only the TD error at $s_1$ updates the shared weight; the target is $0+\gamma(2w)$. Let $\delta$ be this transition's TD error and $w^+$ the updated weight. With constant step size $\alpha>0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta &= 0+\gamma(2w)-w=(2\gamma-1)w,\\
w^+ &= w+\alpha\delta\,\phi(s_1)\\
    &= [1+\alpha(2\gamma-1)]w.
\end{aligned}
$$

If $\gamma>\tfrac12$, the multiplier exceeds 1. For $\gamma=0.9$, $\alpha=0.1$ and initial weight $w_0=1$, after $n$ replay updates $w_n=1.08^n$: estimates grow although all rewards are 0. Updating $w$ to raise the first value also raises the second, making the next target still larger. This is exact arithmetic for the specified toy, not measured training data.

Here off-policy means **biased replay states rather than target-policy visitation**; with one action there is no action-choice mismatch. Replay never corrects the second state's value directly. It fails state coverage, so it does not satisfy the tabular convergence conditions above. This counterexample isolates a possible mechanism; it does not say every combination of all three ingredients diverges. [The primary paper's two-state construction and discussion](https://arxiv.org/html/1812.02648#S2).

</section>
