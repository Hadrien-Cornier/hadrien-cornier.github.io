The first reinforcement learning problem most people hear about is the multi-armed bandit. Imagine a casino with ten slot machines, each paying a hidden average plus some noise. You have a thousand pulls, and you want to win as much as you can.

A bandit has one situation: the same ten machines on every pull. Most problems have many situations, and the reward often shows up long after the move that earned it. A game of chess pays out only when it ends, yet the first move already matters. Rewards can also come at every step, like the cost of 1 per step in the grid of part 1, but the late reward is the hard case.

So which of your earlier moves deserves the credit? That's the credit assignment problem, and the ideas in this series are answers to it and repairs of earlier answers. The central answer is a value function: instead of waiting for the reward, you keep a number for how much reward to expect from here.

## The family tree

Here's the map. Each box is a method, and the label on each line is the failure of the box above that the box below repairs. It's drawn from the family tree in [Breaking Bellman](https://hadrien-cornier.github.io/breaking-bellman/), the small game I made to play with these ideas, and it's a teaching order, not a timeline of who invented what.

How to read it:

- **Trial and error.** Keep an average per machine and pull the best one. This locks in: greedy never retries a machine that looked bad once. ε-greedy, optimistic starts and UCB each repair that.
- **Monte Carlo.** Add situations, and credit every move with all the rewards that followed it. It fails when an episode is long or never ends.
- **Q-learning (part 1).** Learn after every step, from your own guess of what is left. It fails in four ways, one per line out of its box: learning is slow (n-step TD), it falls off the cliff while it explores (Sarsa, part 4), the largest of noisy numbers runs high (Double Q-learning), and with a network in place of the table the numbers can blow up (the triad, repaired by DQN).
- **Bootstrapping (part 2).** Using your own guess as a target is what lets Q-learning learn without waiting. n-step TD sits between that and Monte Carlo.
- **Function approximation and DQN (part 3).** A formula replaces the table when there are too many situations to give each its own row. DQN keeps a network of this kind steady with replayed old moves and a frozen copy.
- **The deadly triad (part 5).** A network, a guess as a target and old data together can make the numbers blow up. That's the "triad" line from Q-learning to DQN.

## The bandit: one situation

Start at the top of the tree. You keep one number per machine, the average payout you've seen, and nudge it after every pull:

$$Q(a) \leftarrow Q(a) + \tfrac{1}{n}\,\bigl(r - Q(a)\bigr)$$

Here $Q(a)$ is the number for machine $a$, $r$ is the payout you just got and $n$ counts the pulls of that machine, so the number ends up as the plain average of its payouts. Below, two robots keep the same table and differ in one rule.

Why the first robot gets stuck:

- **Every number starts at zero.** The first pull goes to a random machine.
- **One good payout is enough.** If it pays anything above zero, its number rises above all the others, which still read zero.
- **The others never get pulled.** Their numbers never get a chance to correct, so the robot keeps the first machine that stayed above zero.

Averaged over 200 runs, the greedy robot spent about a third of its last 200 pulls on the best machine, and the robot with random tries spent about three quarters. It isn't all of them because one pull in ten is random on purpose.

Look at the table itself. It has one row and ten columns, so nothing in it says what to do where. Part 1 adds the situations.

## Return, value and policy

The rest of the series uses five words, and two small maps show how they connect.

- **Reward** $R_{t+1}$: the signal you get after a step.
- **Return** $G_t$: the rewards from now on, added up, with each later one multiplied by one more factor of $\gamma$, a number between 0 and 1 called the discount.
- **Policy** $\pi$: the rule that picks an action in each situation.
- **State value** $v_\pi(s)$: the return you expect from situation $s$ if you follow $\pi$.
- **Action value** $q_\pi(s,a)$: the return you expect if you take action $a$ in $s$ and then follow $\pi$.

Two lines carry most of the weight:

- **For a given policy, the value is an average.** $v_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\, q_\pi(s,a)$, the action values weighted by how often $\pi$ picks each action.
- **For the best values, it's a max.** $v_*(s) = \max_a q_*(s,a)$, and the best policy picks $\arg\max_a q_*(s,a)$ in every situation.

That second line is why a table of action values is enough to act: the largest number in a row is the value of that situation, and the move that holds it is what you do.

## Where the Bellman equation comes from

Everything above hangs on one identity about returns. I use the indexing of Sutton and Barto, where the reward for the move at step $t$ is $R_{t+1}$, so their equation numbers match. Start with the return, written out (their Eq. 3.8):

$$\begin{aligned}G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \cdots \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1}\end{aligned}$$

Take $\gamma$ out of every term after the first. What's left in the brackets is the return from the next step (Eq. 3.9):

$$\begin{aligned}G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma \bigl( R_{t+2} + \gamma R_{t+3} + \cdots \bigr) \\ &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G_{t+1}\end{aligned}$$

If an episode ends at step $T$, there's nothing left to collect, so $G_T = 0$ and the value of the terminal state is 0.

Now take the average. The value of a state under $\pi$ is the expected return from it (Eq. 3.12), and the action value is the same with a first move fixed (Eq. 3.13):

$$\begin{aligned}v_\pi(s) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s\right] \\ q_\pi(s,a) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s,\, A_t = a\right]\end{aligned}$$

Put the factored return inside the average. The average of $G_{t+1}$, given where you land, is $v_\pi$ of that place, so the future collapses into one number (Eq. 3.14, in expectation form):

$$v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[R_{t+1} + \gamma\, v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s\right]$$

- **$\mathbb{E}_\pi[\cdot \mid S_t = s]$** averages over two things: the action that $\pi$ picks in $s$, and what the world does next.
- **$S_t$, $A_t$** are the situation and the action at step $t$. **$R_{t+1}$** is the reward that follows.
- **$\gamma$** is the discount. **$G_t$** is the return. **$v_\pi$** and **$q_\pi$** are the state and action values.

The value isn't a new reward. $R_{t+1}$ is the real reward of one step, and $\gamma\, v_\pi(S_{t+1})$ stands in for everything after it.

That equation only evaluates a policy you already have. To ask for the best behavior, define the best values $v_*(s) = \max_\pi v_\pi(s)$ and $q_*(s,a) = \max_\pi q_\pi(s,a)$ (Eq. 3.15 and 3.16). They satisfy the same recursion with one change: the average over the policy's actions becomes a max over actions (Eq. 3.20):

$$\begin{aligned}q_*(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[&R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} q_*(S_{t+1}, a') \\ &\Bigm|\, S_t = s,\, A_t = a\Bigr]\end{aligned}$$

Here $a'$ in the max ranges over the moves available in the next situation $S_{t+1}$. This is the Bellman optimality equation, and Q-learning (part 1) turns it into an update. The game I made to play with these ideas, Breaking Bellman, is named after it.

## Comparing the methods

The tree has many boxes. This table covers the ones that the five parts after this one take apart.

| Method | Repairs | Assumes | Fails when | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Q-learning (part 1) | Waiting for an episode to end | A table with one number per situation and move, every move tried again and again | The table is too big, random tries cost falls, a max over noisy numbers runs high | [Watkins and Dayan 1992](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/cjch.pdf) |
| n-step TD and bootstrapping (part 2) | Slow one-step guesses and noisy full returns | A guess is a fair target | Each row waits for its n rewards | [Sutton and Barto, ch. 7](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf) |
| Function approximation and DQN (part 3) | A table with a row for every situation, and the divergence of Q-learning with a network | Similar situations have similar values, and replayed old moves with a frozen copy keep a network steady | The numbers run high on some tasks, it needs many samples, and only discrete moves work | [Mnih et al. 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14236) |
| Sarsa and Expected Sarsa (part 4) | Q-learning's falls on the cliff | The target uses the move you really take next | One random next move makes the target noisy, and Expected Sarsa needs every action's probability | [Sutton and Barto, 6.4 to 6.6](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf) |
| Double Q-learning | The upward bias of a max | Two tables that learn from different updates | Each table learns from half the updates | [van Hasselt 2010](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2010/hash/091d584fced301b442654dd8c23b3fc9-Abstract.html) |
| The deadly triad (part 5) | Nothing: it's the failure | A formula, a guess as a target and old data together | The numbers blow up | [Sutton and Barto, 11.3](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf) |
