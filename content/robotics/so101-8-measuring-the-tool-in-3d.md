How do I get the precise x, y, and z coordinates of the tip of my robot's gripper?

My SO-101 is a small robot arm whose gripper currently holds a pen. A Wacom drawing tablet reports where the pen's tip, the nib, touches its surface. That gives me x and y. What I'm missing is z, the height above the plane. I use this setup to build a smooth, precise controller, the program that turns a desired path into motor commands. Drawing is one showcase of the drawing arm project, but the controller should also work in space. That is why I want to move beyond the XY plane.

<div class="rc-mark" id="fig-plane" data-panel="plane" data-title="The tablet sees only its plane"></div>

The tablet is also the best deal in my setup. It cost about US$40 and reports the pen position to within ±0.25 mm, 133 times a second. OptiTrack, a motion-capture system, lists ±0.3 mm for its cameras, and four of them cost about US$10,000 before software. The two figures come from different kinds of specification, but they sit in the same ballpark. That makes the tablet a cheap, precise feedback signal for learning precise motions, as long as the pen touches it.

## Why measure the tip in space?

Tracking the tip's path lets me see how a change to the controller affects the motion. On the tablet, that tells me how well the arm draws. But the arm should also carry a tool above the table, so I want to measure its motion in free space and see whether corrections learned while drawing still help there. Those measurements also give me data to build a model of how the arm moves.

The encoders already tell me each joint angle, giving me some information about the arm's pose. But bending between a joint and the tip can change the path. An outside reference would measure the result of the whole chain, including the tool holder.

## Three ways to measure in space

A friend who works on robots described three routes for measuring beyond the tablet plane. One uses a camera to locate a ChArUco board, a checkerboard with coded markers. Another puts markers on the arm and tracks them with sweeping base stations or infrared motion-capture cameras. The third uses a laser tracker that follows a reflector.

### A camera and a ChArUco board

<div class="rc-mark" id="fig-camera" data-panel="camera" data-title="A camera and a ChArUco target"></div>

The codes on a ChArUco board tell the camera which part of the pattern it's looking at. The corners give it geometric reference points, and the known square size gives their spacing. Once the camera is calibrated, relating image coordinates to rays in space, I can use that known geometry to estimate the board's position and orientation. [OpenCV ChArUco guide](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/df/d4a/tutorial_charuco_detection.html)

<div class="rc-slot" data-for="fig-camera" data-src="/assets/robotics/so101-series/part8/camera.svg" data-caption="The camera measures two patterns: one that never moves and one that rides on the tool. Their relative pose, plus the known offset, gives the nib."><span class="rc-sr">A camera locates a ChArUco target on the tool. Side view. A phone camera looks at a reference board fixed beside the tablet and at a ChArUco target on the tool. Rays run from the lens to each pattern. A known offset connects the target to the nib.</span></div>

In the sketch, a board beside the tablet sets a reference frame, and a target attached to the tool moves with it. The relation between the target and the nib is fixed, so I can use the target's pose to find the nib position. Several calibrated views add a triangulation baseline and help keep the target visible as the arm moves.
Higher image resolution puts more samples across the target, but the geometry matters too. A sideways movement shifts the pattern across the image, while a change in depth changes its apparent scale. That makes the depth estimate sensitive to target size, viewing angle, and lens calibration.

<div class="rc-mark" id="fig-pinhole" data-panel="pinhole" data-title="Depth changes the target size"></div>

<div class="rc-slot" data-for="fig-pinhole" data-src="/assets/robotics/so101-series/part8/pinhole.svg" data-caption="A target of fixed size S makes a longer mark on the image plane when it is near (thick mark) than when it is far (thin mark). A sideways move shifts the mark along the plane instead."><span class="rc-sr">Depth changes the size of the target in the image. Side view of a pinhole camera. A target of the same size makes a longer mark on the image plane when it is near than when it is far.</span></div>

For a small arm, I can use the tablet as a direct reference while drawing and a calibrated camera to measure above the surface. A phone such as the Pixel makes this possible without a dedicated motion-capture installation.

### Markers and base stations or motion-capture cameras

<div class="rc-mark" id="fig-stations" data-panel="stations" data-title="Sweeping base stations"></div>

The second route follows markers on the arm. Sweeping base stations send infrared laser scans across photodiodes on a tracker, and the system combines these observations with inertial measurements to estimate the tracker's position and orientation.
[SteamVR study](https://doi.org/10.3390/s23020725)

<div class="rc-slot" data-for="fig-stations" data-src="/assets/robotics/so101-series/part8/stations.svg" data-caption="Each base station flashes, then sweeps a laser plane across the room. The tracker notes when each photodiode is hit, and that delay becomes an angle. Angles from two stations place the tracker in space."><span class="rc-sr">Sweeping base stations locate a tracker with laser sweeps. Side view. Two base stations on the left and right send sweeping laser planes across a tracker with photodiodes on the tool. The time of each hit gives an angle from each station.</span></div>

Infrared motion capture works differently. Cameras observe reflective markers and triangulate their positions, and a rigid cluster of non-collinear markers also gives the tool's orientation. [OptiTrack marker tracking](https://www.optitrack.com/cameras/primex-13)

<div class="rc-mark" id="fig-mocap" data-panel="mocap" data-title="Infrared motion capture"></div>

<div class="rc-slot" data-for="fig-mocap" data-src="/assets/robotics/so101-series/part8/mocap.svg" data-caption="Cameras triangulate each reflective marker from several rays. Three or more markers fixed to the tool also give its orientation. Single markers on the links show where the arm bends."><span class="rc-sr">Infrared motion capture triangulates reflective markers. Side view. Three cameras look at a rigid cluster of reflective markers on the tool and at single markers on the arm links. Rays from the cameras cross at each marker.</span></div>

A tracker can look stable while stationary and still have substantially larger errors when it moves. In the SteamVR study, the four-station setup reached [0.574 mm aggregate RMSE at 12.5 mm/s, rising to 4.237 mm at 50 mm/s](https://doi.org/10.3390/s23020725). RMSE stands for root mean square error and gives large misses more weight than small ones. Since I want to track a moving tool, the dynamic result matters.

Marker placement determines what the measurement includes. Put markers near the tool to measure its motion. Put them on separate arm links too, and they can also reveal where the chain bends.

### A laser tracker

<div class="rc-mark" id="fig-laser" data-panel="laser" data-title="A laser tracker"></div>

A laser tracker combines a distance measurement with angular encoders to locate the center of a reflector in space. A dedicated pose target adds orientation, which matters when the reflector is offset from a rotating tool tip. [Hexagon tracker specifications](https://solutions-ds.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Hexagon_MI_AT960_Brochure_EN.pdf)

<div class="rc-slot" data-for="fig-laser" data-src="/assets/robotics/so101-series/part8/laser.svg" data-caption="The tracker tracks a reflector with its beam. Interferometry or a distance meter gives the distance d. Two angular encoders give the elevation β and the azimuth α. Together they fix the reflector center in space."><span class="rc-sr">A laser tracker measures distance and two angles to a reflector. Side view with a top-view inset. A laser tracker on a stand sends a beam to a reflector on the tool. It measures the beam distance d, the elevation angle beta, and the azimuth angle alpha.</span></div>

This is industrial dimensional measurement equipment, providing an independent metric reference with tight specified error bounds. The target and its mounting geometry are part of the measurement system too.

### What the US$40 buys

Inside its plane, the tablet quotes accuracy in the same ballpark as a motion-capture system that costs more than 200 times as much.

- **Accuracy:** the tablet lists ±0.25 mm and OptiTrack PrimeX 13 lists ±0.3 mm. The tablet's figure is a tolerance bound and OptiTrack's is a typical value, so they are comparable quoted specifications, not a measured tie.
- **Price:** the tablet cost me about US$40. Four PrimeX 13 cameras cost US$9,996 at list price, plus US$1,148 for the Motive:Tracker software and key.
- **Resolution:** Wacom lists 100 lines per mm, which is 0.01 mm. That is much finer than its accuracy, so the pen reports small moves that the sensor does not claim to place exactly.

The tablet does not win everywhere. OptiTrack tracks in three dimensions and samples at 240 Hz, compared with the tablet's 133. The Leica laser tracker has a specified error bound of 0.021 mm at 1 m, more than ten times tighter than the tablet's, and costs US$17k to 75k.

## From the sensor target to the nib

<div class="rc-mark" id="fig-chain" data-panel="chain" data-title="From the sensor target to the nib"></div>

<div class="rc-slot" data-for="fig-chain" data-src="/assets/robotics/so101-series/part8/chain.svg" data-caption="Whatever sensor you pick, it reports the target pose: position t and rotation R. The offset r is fixed in the target frame, so the nib follows from p = t + R r. A small angle error turns the offset and shifts the nib estimate."><span class="rc-sr">From the sensor target to the nib. Side view. The fixed frame origin, the target position t, and the offset r turned by the target rotation R add up to the nib position p. A small rotation error moves the estimated nib.</span></div>

These systems track a target near the nib, so I still need the geometric relation between what the sensor sees and the point I care about. If the target position is $t$, its rotation is $R$, and the nib offset in target coordinates is $r$, then

$$
p_{\text{nib}} = t + Rr.
$$

Now consider an orientation error. It rotates the offset and shifts the estimated nib position. Keeping the target close to the nib reduces that lever arm. A delayed sample creates another error: it describes where the tool was, and speed turns the delay into apparent path error.

## Limits

- **Camera depth is the weakest point of the accessible route.** Image resolution alone does not establish depth accuracy, and lens calibration, rolling shutter, occlusion, target orientation, and holder flex all affect the result. The ChArUco paper measured a different calibration rig than a Pixel tracking this arm.
- **The reference point and the error metric matter.** The tablet has its own tolerance and supplies no calibrated height or tilt. Camera and tracker figures apply to their targets, and a rigid offset cannot account for flex between target and nib. Accuracy, repeatability, and the table's error metrics describe different properties.
- **The quoted conditions differ.** SteamVR results include coordinate and sample-rate post-processing. OptiTrack's product page and [family sheet, which lists ±0.2 mm](https://optitrack-documents.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/primex-family-spec-sheet-26.01.16.pdf), differ. Leica's bound uses specified precision reflectors. None of these figures establishes nib accuracy on this arm.
- **Rate is not latency.** OptiTrack's listed camera latency excludes the rest of the application, and the other cited sources do not establish end-to-end delay for the complete recording chains. Tracking volume also depends on visibility, and component prices exclude a complete installation.

## Which route fits a small arm?

For a small arm like the SO-101, a calibrated camera offers an accessible way to measure beyond the drawing plane. A phone already provides the images, and coded targets give me a geometric reference. The tablet stays useful on the surface, where it measures the pen directly.

Sweeping base stations offer wider coverage with compact tracking hardware, though their dynamic error matters for precise paths. Optical motion capture provides synchronized views and rigid-body tracking, but requires a dedicated installation. A laser tracker fits work that needs an independent dimensional reference. The choice depends on the motion I want to measure, the point each system tracks, and the conditions behind its accuracy figures.
