Run a recorded motion (it is called <code>cat</code>) on a real arm this way, and on average the joints end up 22.5 mrad away from where they were asked to be. That is about 1.3°, on an arm whose gripper reaches about 30 cm.</p><p>So the servo is told an angle and goes somewhere else. I like this puzzle because we get just one number to work with: the angle we send. How much can we fix with that? To answer, we need to pull apart four angles that the default setup treats as one:</p><ul><li><strong>target</strong> q*: where we want the joint, from the policy or a planned path;</li><li><strong>goal</strong> g: the angle we actually send to the servo;</li><li><strong>physical angle</strong> q: where the joint really is;</li><li><strong>reading</strong> q̂: what the encoder reports, in integer ticks.</li></ul><p>The default controller (LeRobot's <code>direct</code>) sets g = q* at every update. Tracking error is how far the joint ends up from q*. The question for this article: what g should we send instead?</p><figure class='so101-video'><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/so101-series/cat-direct.png'><source src='/assets/robotics/so101-series/cat-direct.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/so101-series/cat-direct.mp4'>Open the cat-direct video</a></video><figcaption>Real SO-101 with the default <code>direct</code> controller at 30 Hz, replaying the recorded <code>cat</code> joint angles. One degree is 17.45 mrad, or about 11.4 encoder ticks, and one tick is 1.534 mrad (0.088°). A torque divided by a stiffness is an angle: N·m ÷ (N·m/rad) = rad. A damping divided by a stiffness is a time: (N·m·s/rad) ÷ (N·m/rad) = s.</p></details><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='spring-goal' data-view='spring-goal' data-title='Every torque costs angle'></div><span id="section-what-s-next" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-target-goal-gap" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><h2 id='section-spring'>A position servo is a spring you can move</h2><p>Start with the simplest servo there is: proportional control. It looks at the gap between the goal and the joint and pushes with a torque proportional to it, τ = k<sub>p</sub> (g − q). That is a torsional spring. You hold one end at angle g, the other end is bolted to the joint, and moving g drags the joint along. The stiffness k<sub>p</sub> is in N·m/rad. For the worked example I'll use the simulator's model of the STS3215 servo (fitted for the 12 V motor by the open-source BAM project, at LeRobot's setting P = 16), where k<sub>p</sub> = 13.64 N·m/rad. The simulator lets us switch physics on and off one term at a time. The real servo differs, and we'll line the two up near the end.</p><div class='so101-predict' data-so101-widget='predict' data-id='S1'><p><strong>Predict.</strong> Target and goal are both −0.321 rad, friction is zero, and gravity pulls on the shoulder with 0.391 N·m. Where does the joint settle?</p><ol class='so101-options'><li data-correct='false'>Exactly at −0.321 rad</li><li data-correct='true'>28.7 mrad away, on the gravity side</li><li data-correct='false'>Nowhere: it keeps oscillating</li></ol><details><summary>Answer</summary><p>28.7 mrad on the gravity side, which is G/k<sub>p</sub>.</p></details></div><p>Now hold the shoulder out in the air, where gravity pulls on it with G = 0.391 N·m. A spring only pushes when it is stretched, so for the joint to stand still the gap has to be G/k<sub>p</sub> = 0.391 / 13.64 = 0.0287 rad, or 28.7 mrad. With g = q*, all of that stretch comes out of the joint: it sags 28.7 mrad below the target and stays there. The servo is doing its job perfectly. It's a spring, and a loaded spring stretches.</p><p>And now the fix is obvious: aim high. Send g = q* + G/k<sub>p</sub> and the spring gets its 28.7 mrad of stretch with the joint sitting exactly on target (as long as we know G and there is no friction, which we'll come back to). Keep this move in mind, because the rest of the article repeats it. Every torque the joint needs, to hold, to accelerate or to push through friction, has to come from a gap, and on this servo a gap costs about 73 mrad per N·m (that is 1/k<sub>p</sub>). If we know a torque in advance, we can put its angle into the goal. If we don't, it shows up as tracking error.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='two-loops' data-view='two-loops' data-title='Two loops, one knob'></div><span id="section-what-pid-actually-means" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-the-servo-sits-in-the-middle" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><span id="section-the-servo-side" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><h2 id='section-inside-the-servo'>Inside the servo, and the one knob we get</h2><p>Why not just make the servo try harder? An STS3215 is a small closed box: motor, gearbox, encoder and an embedded controller. The embedded controller runs a fast loop (its exact rate is not documented) that compares the encoder with the goal and sets motor power. Our code runs a second, slower loop outside the box, at 30 or 60 Hz, and the only thing it can do is write a new goal. Everything in this article happens through g.</p><p>The inner loop is a PID controller, and each part has a physical meaning. P is the spring stiffness we just met. D opposes velocity, so it behaves like damping. I adds up error over time, and given time it would remove the gravity sag by itself. LeRobot connects every motor with P = 16, I = 0, D = 32 (and caps the gripper's torque at 50%, which its code attributes to burnout protection). With I = 0, nothing inside the box removes sag, so that job is ours. The reason for I = 0 is not documented. My guess is that it avoids integral windup on a gripper squeezing an object, which would hold full torque and heat the motor.</p><details><summary>What P, D and I do to a gravity-loaded step</summary><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-series/servo-inside.svg' alt='The STS3215 opened up: motor, gearbox, encoder and embedded controller' loading='lazy'><img src='/assets/robotics/so101-series/pid-parts.svg' alt='Three step responses under gravity: P alone, P plus D, P plus I' loading='lazy'><p>This is a teaching model, not an identification of the hardware. The P torque is 13.64(g − q); P+D subtracts the velocity v; P+I adds 20∫e dt. P alone oscillates and keeps its sag. Adding D settles smoothly but keeps the sag. Adding I removes the sag eventually, with oscillation on the way.</p></details><div class='so101-predict' data-so101-widget='predict' data-id='D1'><p><strong>Predict.</strong> Which of these can a controller read from the arm's calibration file?</p><ol class='so101-options'><li data-correct='true'>Where each joint's zero is and how far it may move</li><li data-correct='false'>Each joint's stiffness and damping</li><li data-correct='false'>Each joint's friction and delay</li></ol><details><summary>Answer</summary><p>Only the geometry: the zero position and the range.</p></details></div><p>What about calibration? The calibration file, <code>hadrien_follower.json</code>, holds five integers per motor: <code>id</code>, <code>drive_mode</code>, <code>homing_offset</code>, <code>range_min</code> and <code>range_max</code>. For <code>shoulder_lift</code> the homing offset is 941 ticks and the range runs from 844 to 3214 ticks. That is geometry: where zero is and how far the joint may go. Nothing in it describes this arm's stiffness, damping, friction, delay or gravity, and every motor gets the same servo settings. The physics is ours to supply.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='servo-equation' data-view='servo-equation' data-title='The joint in one equation'></div><span id="section-one-equation-for-the-joint" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><h2 id='section-one-equation'>The whole joint in one line</h2><p>Follow one goal around the loop:</p><p class='so101-flow' data-so101-widget='servo-equation'>target q* → goal g that we send → after dead time D, servo torque k<sub>p</sub> [g − q̂] → joint angle q → encoder reading q̂ in ticks → back to the servo</p><p>Now write the spring idea out for everything the shoulder has to pay for:</p><p class='rc-eq'>k<sub>p</sub> [ g(t − D) − q̂ ] = M(q) q̈ + G(q) + d q̇ + f sign(q̇) + C(q, q̇) q̇</p><p>The left side is the servo: a spring of stiffness k<sub>p</sub> stretched between the goal and the encoder reading, except that the goal it acts on was sent D seconds ago. That D is dead time, the pause before the servo responds at all. On the right is everything the spring pays for. We already know it has to hold the arm against gravity, G(q). Moving adds more work. We have to get the links accelerating, push against damping and dry friction, and handle the forces that one moving link puts on another. Those are the other terms on the right. We'll take them one at a time. Two actuator effects, the dead band and the torque limit, don't fit inside the equality, and they get their own section.</p><p>Now do what we did with gravity. If we know the target path q*(t), we know its velocity and acceleration too. Put the target in place of the joint on the right side and solve for the goal:</p><p class='rc-eq'>g(t) = q*(t + D) + [ M q̈* + G + d q̇* + f sign(q̇*) + C q̇* ] / k<sub>p</sub></p><p>The bracket is the torque the target motion needs, turned into angle. The shift to t + D sends each goal early so that it lands on time. This is feedforward: it uses only what we know about the target, and it is exact when the model terms are exact. The derivatives come from the planned path, never from the encoder (the encoder section shows why). Whatever the model misses still shows up as measured tracking error, and correcting that is the job of feedback.</p><p>The terms come in three families that fail in different ways: the actuator, the mechanics and the sensing. Near zero gap the real servo has a dead band: if the gap is inside it, the motor does nothing. Fits on the hardware put it at 8.1–21.0 mrad depending on the joint, about 5 to 14 ticks, and outside it the servo pushes as if the gap were shorter by the band. So a goal offset that works while the joint moves one way must change by twice the band when it reverses. The simulator we started from has no dead band.</p><h3 id='section-torque-limit'>Torque limit</h3><p>At the other edge, torque saturates. The simulator clamps it at 5.107 N·m, about 5.1. Past that point, pushing the goal further changes nothing, so no choice of g produces more torque. The defense is to plan motions that never ask for it, for example by braking earlier and more gently. As an illustration, on a joint with J = 0.06 kg·m², a late stop might need 7 N·m where an early one needs 3.</p><h3 id='section-heat'>Heat</h3><p>Heat lowers the clamp. Copper's resistance rises about 0.4% per °C, so at a fixed supply voltage the current and the torque fall, and the magnets weaken as they warm. An illustrative servo delivers about 82% of its 25 °C torque at 80 °C. The STS3215 also has an over-temperature cutoff. None of this was measured on the arm, and the simulator has no temperature model. Our goal offset has to account for that.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='friction-band' data-view='friction-band' data-title='Static friction makes a band'></div><span id="section-why-the-simulator-creeps" data-legacy-anchor=""></span><h2 id='section-dry-friction-makes-a-band'>Static friction turns a point into a band</h2><p>While the joint moves, dry friction is easy: a constant f = 0.196 N·m against the velocity, the same at any speed, added on top of damping. Standing still is where it gets interesting. The friction on a stuck joint is whatever torque between −f and +f keeps it stuck.</p><p>Put that into the balance at rest, k<sub>p</sub> · gap + F = G with F anywhere in [−f, +f], and the gap becomes a range: (G − f)/k<sub>p</sub> to (G + f)/k<sub>p</sub>, or 14.3 to 43.0 mrad. The joint can also move a little before it starts sliding, and its friction can depend on how it moved a moment ago. LuGre models that behavior by keeping an internal state. And friction can grow with load, F = F<sub>c</sub> + μ|τ|.</p><p>Does the extra machinery help? A stiffer servo at 22.1 N·m/rad (×1.62) gives 24.8 mrad, where scaling all of the error by 1/k<sub>p</sub> would predict about 23. Fit those two results with one part that shrinks as stiffness rises and a fixed part left over: 32.2 × 13.64/k<sub>p</sub> + 5.0 mrad. Now test the fit on a weaker supply, with stiffness 12.31 N·m/rad: it predicts 40.6 mrad, and the simulation gives 40.5. The 5 mrad that refuses to scale is the command-hold floor.</p><p>Turning the stiffness up further runs into everything else. Dead time stays. Torque saturates at 5.1 N·m. Each encoder tick becomes a torque step of k<sub>p</sub> × 1.534 mrad. Delayed feedback turns high gain into alternating overshoot and chatter: in one reported test, an outer gain of 4 was stable and a gain of 100 oscillated. Past a point, more gain buys less accuracy and then buys instability.</p><div class='rc-mark' data-panel='spring-goal' data-view='whole' data-title='The whole board'></div><h2 id='section-which-goal'>So, which goal?</h2><p>Send the target shifted D ahead, plus every torque the motion needs divided by the stiffness. Sending goals more often trims the 12 mrad of command hold, and you can drop the speed-squared terms. The error that remains tells you what is missing: terms you left out of the model, parameters you got wrong, the encoder's ticks, and the distance between the simulated servo and the real one. <a href='/robotics/so101-2-finer-than-the-sensor/'>Part 2</a> reads the joint more finely than the sensor, <a href='/robotics/so101-3-choosing-the-goal/'>part 3</a> builds this goal formula into a controller and chooses which terms to compensate, and <a href='/robotics/so101-4-learning-what-physics-misses/'>part 4</a> learns what the physics misses.</p></div></div>
