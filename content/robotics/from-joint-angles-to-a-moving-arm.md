It swings there and holds against a load. How does a number become controlled motion? And how do measured angles tell us where the gripper is and which way it points? We'll follow a toy arm with two links of length 1 and 0.8, shoulder at 30°, elbow at 60°.</p><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='config' data-view='[0,0,600,420]' data-title='Configuration'></span><h2>The robot knows a list of angles</h2><p>The <a href='https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/so101'>SO-101</a> has five arm joints plus a gripper actuator, and each smart servo measures its own angle. A Franka Panda has seven rotary arm joints plus sliding fingers. Stack the angles in one list, q = (q₁, …, q₇): that is the <strong>configuration</strong>, and the space of all such lists is <strong>joint space</strong>. The links are rigid with known shapes, so q fixes where every link sits and how it is turned.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_joint_names.png' alt='Franka Panda with its seven arm joints named and their rotation axes marked' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>The Panda's seven arm joints, each with the axis it turns about.</figcaption></figure><p>Turn only the Panda's wrist: the joint centers stay put and the gripper can keep pointing the same way, yet the hand rolls about its own axis. Joint centers or a pointing direction miss that roll.</p><figure><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_wrist_motion.png' style='max-width:100%'><source src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_wrist_motion.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_wrist_motion.mp4'>Open the video</a></video><figcaption>Simulated Panda: only the wrist turns while the upstream joints stay fixed. (<a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_wrist_motion.mp4'>MP4</a>)</figcaption></figure><p>q is a snapshot, though. Two arms at (30°, 60°), one still and one swinging fast, need very different braking in the next instant. So the <strong>mechanical state</strong> is the pair (q, q̇): angles plus velocities. A robot that senses its own joints is doing <a href='https://www.ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=proprioception'>proprioception</a> (Latin <em>proprius</em>, one's own).</p><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='encoder' data-view='[600,0,600,420]' data-title='Reading one angle'></span><h2>How a servo reads its own angle</h2><p>Take the <a href='https://www.infineon.com/assets/row/public/documents/24/49/infineon-as5600-datasheet-en.pdf'>AS5600</a>, a chip that sits under a small magnet on the motor shaft and reads the magnet's angle. The field pushes sideways on charges moving through the chip's <a href='https://www.ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/sszt164'>Hall elements</a>, which makes a small voltage, and the electronics turn this into two signed signals a quarter turn apart. Those get digitized, and the angle comes from them:</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>X = A cos θ,   Y = A sin θ</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/magnet_chip_signal_path.png' alt='Magnet on the shaft above a Hall sensor chip, and the chain from analog signals to a digital angle' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>Magnet, sensor and the signal chain from voltages to an angle.</figcaption></figure><p>At our 30°, with A = 1, (X, Y) = (0.866, 0.5). Why two signals? Because one is ambiguous: sin 30° = sin 150° = 0.5. The cosines have opposite signs, and that settles it: θ = <a href='https://www.ti.com/lit/ug/tiduc07/tiduc07.pdf'>atan2(Y, X)</a> uses both signs to pick the right quadrant.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/equal_sine_opposite_cosine.png' alt='Angles of 30 and 150 degrees on a circle with the same sine and opposite cosines' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>30° and 150° share a sine of 0.5; their cosines have opposite signs.</figcaption></figure><p>The AS5600 computes atan2 with a hardwired block called CORDIC (Coordinate Rotation Digital Computer), and the trick is lovely. Rotate the vector (X, Y) in the registers (the magnet stays put) until it lies flat on the x-axis, and keep a tally of the turns. The tally is the angle. The clever part is choosing turns whose multiplications become divisions by 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on, which are just shifts in binary. Those stored angles are φᵢ = arctan(2<sup>−i</sup>), and <a href='https://www.st.com/resource/en/application_note/an5325-getting-started-with-the-cordic-accelerator-using-stm32cube-stmicroelectronics.pdf'>each step needs only shifts and adds</a>:</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>x<sub>new</sub> = x + 2<sup>−i</sup> y,   y<sub>new</sub> = y − 2<sup>−i</sup> x   (clockwise; flip both signs to turn back)</p><p>Turn clockwise and add φᵢ when y is positive; turn back and subtract it when y is negative. Each step is an exact rotation plus a stretch, so error comes only from stopping after finitely many steps and from rounding the stored numbers.</p><p>Is that the fast way, though? I <a href='https://github.com/Hadrien-Cornier/maniskill-playground/tree/main/experiments/encoder-benchmark'>benchmarked</a> three methods, and all three stayed within half a 12-bit encoder step (360°/8192 ≈ 0.0439°). CORDIC beat a basic Taylor series by about 55×. But a Taylor series with extra range reduction was about 6× faster than CORDIC, on native C++ on an Apple M1 Max laptop. A sensor chip wants something else: one small dedicated circuit that repeats shift, add, subtract and lookup. That is CORDIC.</p><details id='section-how-does-the-encoder-find-the-angle'><summary>How the encoder finds the angle: the CORDIC derivation and the benchmark</summary><p>The hardware. A <a href='https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/Grove-12-bit-Magnetic-Rotary-Position-Sensor-AS5600/'>Seeed Grove board</a> with the AS5600 was listed at US$6.50 for one on 2026-09-28, before tax and shipping.</p><p>Why shifts and adds rotate the vector. A clockwise rotation by φ is</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>x<sub>rot</sub> = x cos φ + y sin φ = cos φ (x + y tan φ)<br>y<sub>rot</sub> = y cos φ − x sin φ = cos φ (y − x tan φ)</p><p>For 0° ≤ φ ≤ 45° the common factor cos φ is positive, so dropping it keeps the direction and only stretches the vector. Rotating (1, 0) clockwise by 45° gives (0.707…, −0.707…); the update without cos φ gives (1, −1), the same direction with a greater length. With tan φᵢ = 2<sup>−i</sup>, the remaining factors at i = 0, 1, 2 are 1, 1/2, 1/4, which scaled integers apply as right shifts. Both coordinates update from the old values at the same time.</p><p>The recurrence for the 30° example:</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>x₀ = X,  y₀ = Y,  a₀ = 0;   dᵢ = +1 if yᵢ ≥ 0, otherwise −1<br>x<sub>i+1</sub> = xᵢ + dᵢ 2<sup>−i</sup> yᵢ,   y<sub>i+1</sub> = yᵢ − dᵢ 2<sup>−i</sup> xᵢ,   a<sub>i+1</sub> = aᵢ + dᵢ φᵢ</p><p>The first floating-point step gives x₁ = 1.366, y₁ = −0.366. When y gets close to zero, the tally a is close to the input angle. Inputs anywhere on the full circle need a few extra sign tests and possible negations first. In integers, (887, 512) is about 29.995°. The first update gives (1399, −375); the negative y triggers a reverse turn, and the next shift is 1399 &gt;&gt; 1 = 699, a division by a power of two rounded down.</p><figure><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/cordic_walkthrough_poster.png' style='max-width:100%'><source src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/cordic_walkthrough.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/cordic_walkthrough.mp4'>Open the video</a></video><figcaption>Twelve integer CORDIC steps: old coordinates, shifts, updates, the angle tally and the vector direction. The series arctan z = z − z³/3 + z⁵/5 − … converges slowly near z = 1. Signs and axis swaps reduce any input to α in [0°, 45°], so z is in [0, 1]. The SO-101 servo packs a motor, the encoder, a driver, gears and a controller into one case. The laptop's message reaches it through a <a href='https://docs.waveshare.com/Bus_Servo_Adapter_A/FAQ'>USB adapter</a> and the servo bus, and the <a href='https://files.seeedstudio.com/products/Feetech/108090023_STS3215-C001_Datasheet.pdf'>servo</a> keeps running its own feedback loop between messages. The commanded angle is the action; the encoder reading is an observation of the current state.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/so101_servo_feedback.png' alt='Laptop connected through a USB adapter to the servo bus, with a feedback loop inside each servo' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>The laptop talks to the bus; the feedback runs inside each servo.</figcaption></figure><p>Say the target is q<sub>d</sub> = 30° and the encoder reads q = 20°, so the error is e = q<sub>d</sub> − q = 10°. The standard controller model here is PID (I have not checked the servo's exact firmware):</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>u(t) = K<sub>P</sub> e(t) + K<sub>I</sub> ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> e(s) ds + K<sub>D</sub> ė(t)</p><p>u is the motor drive the controller asks for, and the K gains set how strong each correction is. P (proportional) pushes on the error right now. I (integral) pushes on the error summed over time, so it keeps pushing after the error reaches zero. D (derivative) reacts to how fast the error changes; with a fixed target ė = −q̇, so D brakes harder the faster the joint moves.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/03_pid_terms.png' alt='Error curve showing the present error, the accumulated area and the slope used by the three PID terms' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>What each term reads: the error now, the area under it so far, and its slope.</figcaption></figure><p>Why bother with I? Hang a constant load on the joint. P only pushes when there is error, so the joint settles short of the target, where K<sub>P</sub> e balances the load. The integral grows while that gap lasts and keeps its value once the gap closes, so it ends up holding the load at the <a href='https://modernrobotics.northwestern.edu/nu-gm-book-resource/11-4-motion-control-with-torque-or-force-inputs-part-2-of-3/'>target</a>.</p><figure><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/why_integral_helps.png' style='max-width:100%'><source src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/why_integral_helps.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/why_integral_helps.mp4'>Open the video</a></video><figcaption>Schematic simulation: the integral accumulates until it supports a 2 N·m load at the target. (<a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/why_integral_helps.mp4'>MP4</a>)</figcaption></figure><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='ablation' data-view='[600,420,600,420]' data-title='Remove one term'></span><h3>Remove one term at a time</h3><p>I checked these roles in a <a href='https://github.com/Hadrien-Cornier/maniskill-playground/tree/main/experiments/pid-ablation'>simulation</a> of one link turning in a horizontal plane. It starts at rest at 0°, and we ask for 45.8° while a constant 2 N·m torque pushes against it. Each run removes one controller term and keeps everything else the same. The board compares the outcomes. Without D, the link's own friction eventually calms the ringing; the growing swings without P belong to these particular gains.</p><figure><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/pid_ablation_comparison.png' style='max-width:100%'><source src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/pid_ablation_comparison.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/pid_ablation_comparison.mp4'>Open the video</a></video><figcaption>Full PID, no I, no D and no P side by side: 24 simulated seconds at 2× playback. (<a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v4/pid_ablation_comparison.mp4'>MP4</a>)</figcaption></figure><p>The simulation drives torque directly with no limit. Under another load or pose the same gains can behave differently, so some controllers <a href='https://modernrobotics.northwestern.edu/nu-gm-book-resource/11-4-motion-control-with-torque-or-force-inputs-part-1-of-3/'>change gains with the joint, pose or load</a>.</p><details><summary>Simulation setup, checks and damping</summary><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>M q̈ = u − b q̇ − 2,   u = 16 e + 4 ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>t</sup> e(s) ds − 7 q̇</p><p>M = 1 kg·m² and b = 0.5 N·m·s/rad. The angle, velocity and integrated error start at zero, and each ablation sets one gain to zero. Without I, the resting derivative term is zero, so P must supply 2 N·m: with K<sub>P</sub> = 16 that takes 2/16 = 0.125 rad ≈ 7.16° of error. The controller applies continuous torque directly with no torque limit, and the derivative term uses measured velocity so a target change does not cause a kick. The integration step was 2 ms, repeated at 1 ms, and the angles matched the exact linear-system solution within 10<sup>−7</sup> rad. Settling means staying within 2% of the target for the rest of an 80 s run. The system poles confirm the no-P instability. Code, parameters and complete traces are in <a href='https://github.com/Hadrien-Cornier/maniskill-playground/tree/main/experiments/pid-ablation'>pid-ablation</a>.</p><p>Damping. For one joint with a fixed target under PD control:</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>M ë + (b + K<sub>D</sub>) ė + K<sub>P</sub> e = 0,   ζ = (b + K<sub>D</sub>) / (2√(M K<sub>P</sub>))</p><p>ζ is dimensionless. For 0 &lt; ζ &lt; 1 the joint is underdamped and overshoots with oscillation; ζ = 1 is the critical boundary; for ζ &gt; 1 it is overdamped and approaches more slowly without oscillating. This ideal second-order model assumes positive M and K<sub>P</sub> and leaves out integral action, gravity, delay and contact. With the simulation's numbers and the integral ignored, ζ = (0.5 + 7)/(2·4) ≈ 0.94; with K<sub>D</sub> removed, ζ = 0.5/8 ≈ 0.06, deep in the ringing regime.</p><figure><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/damping_joint_responses_poster.png' style='max-width:100%'><source src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/damping_joint_responses.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/damping_joint_responses.mp4'>Open the video</a></video><figcaption>Underdamped, critically damped and overdamped joints moving to the same target. (<a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/damping_joint_responses.mp4'>MP4</a>)</figcaption></figure></details><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='fk' data-view='[0,840,600,420]' data-title='Forward kinematics'></span><h2>From angles to the gripper</h2><p>Now the second question: given q, where is the gripper? That is <strong>forward kinematics</strong> (<a href='https://ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=kinematics'>kinematics</a>: motion without its forces; <a href='https://ahdictionary.com/word/search.html?q=dynamic'>dynamics</a> adds the forces and torques). Each joint angle is measured from the link before, so the link directions add up: α₁ = 30° and α₂ = 30° + 60° = 90°. Each link then adds its length times the cosine and sine of its direction:</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>x = cos q₁ + 0.8 cos(q₁ + q₂) = 0.866 + 0 = 0.866<br>y = sin q₁ + 0.8 sin(q₁ + q₂) = 0.5 + 0.8 = 1.3</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/link_projections_one_two_three.png' alt='One, two and three planar links with cumulative angles and summed link projections' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>One, two and three links: relative angles add into directions, and the projections add into the gripper position.</figcaption></figure><p>The SO-101's bending joints work in a vertical plane, so the same sum gives a signed reach r and a height z. The pan angle φ then swings that plane around the vertical: x = r cos φ, y = r sin φ. Call the whole map p = g(q). It takes a point in joint space to a point in <strong>task space</strong>, where the hand lives. Try it below.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/so101_radial_side_top.png' alt='Simplified SO-101 side view with reach and height, and top view with the pan angle' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>Simplified SO-101: reach r and height z = h + Σ Lᵢ sin αᵢ above a shoulder at height h (side), and the pan angle φ that turns r into x and y (top). r goes negative when the arm folds back past the pan axis, so the horizontal distance is |r|.</figcaption></figure><figure class='arm-widget' data-l1='1' data-l2='0.8'><svg viewBox='0 0 400 330' xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg' style='width:100%;max-width:440px;background:#161b22;border-radius:10px' role='img' aria-label='Two-link arm with shoulder at 30 degrees and elbow at 60 degrees inside its reachable ring'><path d='M56,170 a144,144 0 1,0 288,0 a144,144 0 1,0 -288,0 Z M184,170 a16,16 0 1,0 32,0 a16,16 0 1,0 -32,0 Z' fill='#8bd17c' fill-opacity='0.14' fill-rule='evenodd' stroke='#8bd17c' stroke-opacity='0.5'/><polyline data-part='ghost' points='200,170 210.2,90.7 269.3,66' fill='none' stroke='#6cc4e0' stroke-width='3' stroke-dasharray='6 5'/><polyline data-part='arm' points='200,170 269.3,130 269.3,66' fill='none' stroke='#e8e6df' stroke-width='6' stroke-linecap='round' stroke-linejoin='round'/><circle cx='200' cy='170' r='6' fill='#9aa4b2'/><circle data-part='elbow' cx='269.3' cy='130' r='6' fill='#6cc4e0'/><circle data-part='hand' cx='269.3' cy='66' r='7' fill='#f2b84b'/></svg><div style='display:flex;gap:1rem;flex-wrap:wrap;margin-top:.5rem'><label>Shoulder q₁ <input type='range' min='-180' max='180' step='1' value='30' data-arm='q1'> <output data-out='q1'>30°</output></label><label>Elbow q₂ <input type='range' min='-150' max='150' step='1' value='60' data-arm='q2'> <output data-out='q2'>60°</output></label></div><p data-out='readout'>q = (30°, 60°), hand at (0.866, 1.300). The shaded ring is every hand position it can reach with no joint limits.</figcaption></figure><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='jacobian' data-view='[600,840,600,420]' data-title='The Jacobian'></span><h2>Small turns: the Jacobian</h2><p>Nudge the shoulder a little. Which way does the hand go, and how far? Differentiate g and you get the <a href='https://modernrobotics.northwestern.edu/nu-gm-book-resource/5-1-1-space-jacobian/'>Jacobian</a>: ṗ = J(q) q̇. Column j is the hand velocity from turning joint j alone at unit speed, and for small changes Δp ≈ J(q) Δq. At (30°, 60°):</p><div class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>J(q) = <table style='display:inline-table;vertical-align:middle;border-left:2px solid currentColor;border-right:2px solid currentColor;border-radius:4px;border-collapse:separate;border-spacing:12px 3px;margin:0 .3em'><tr><td>−sin q₁ − 0.8 sin(q₁+q₂)</td><td>−0.8 sin(q₁+q₂)</td></tr><tr><td>cos q₁ + 0.8 cos(q₁+q₂)</td><td>0.8 cos(q₁+q₂)</td></tr></table> = <table style='display:inline-table;vertical-align:middle;border-left:2px solid currentColor;border-right:2px solid currentColor;border-radius:4px;border-collapse:separate;border-spacing:12px 3px;margin:0 .3em'><tr><td>−1.3</td><td>−0.8</td></tr><tr><td>0.866</td><td>0</td></tr></table></div><p>J changes with q. Compare the extended and folded arms below: the same shoulder turn moves the hand different distances.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/jacobian_extended_folded.png' alt='Extended and folded arms after the same shoulder turn, with different gripper displacements' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>The same shoulder turn, extended and folded: the gripper moves different distances.</figcaption></figure><p>At (30°, 60°), well inside the valid region, the two columns point in different directions, so mixing shoulder and elbow turns can move the hand in any direction in the plane. That is what rank 2 means here, and the set of hand velocities you can produce is J's <strong>image</strong>. No nonzero mix leaves the hand still, so J's <strong>kernel</strong>, the joint velocities that hold the hand still to first order, contains only zero. The Panda needs rank 3 for hand position, and with seven joints it has kernel to spare: the elbow can swing while the hand stays put.</p><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='ik' data-view='[0,1260,600,420]' data-title='Inverse kinematics'></span><h2>Going backwards: inverse kinematics</h2><p>Flip the question. Given the target p* = (0.866, 1.3), which q gets there? Our arm has two answers: (30°, 60°), and the mirror image with the elbow on the other side, about (82.6°, −60°). In general, <strong>inverse kinematics</strong> returns a set:</p><p class='rc-eq' style='text-align:center'>{ q ∈ Q<sub>valid</sub> : g(q) = p* }</p><p>Q<sub>valid</sub> applies joint limits and whichever collision checks you chose, so the set can be empty, a few points, or a whole family. Now suppose the solver picks one elbow arrangement for this target and the other one for the next, slightly shifted target. The hand barely needs to move, but the elbow jumps across! Starting each solve from the arm's current angles usually helps it find the nearby answer. And a valid endpoint still says nothing about whether a safe path reaches it.</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_same_pose_two_configs.png' alt='Two simulated Panda configurations with the same gripper pose at a cube and elbows in different places' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>One gripper position and orientation at the cube, two Panda configurations, elbows about 38 cm apart.</figcaption></figure><figure><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/ik_branch_selection_poster.png' style='max-width:100%'><source src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/ik_branch_selection.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/ik_branch_selection.mp4'>Open the video</a></video><figcaption>Alternating between two valid IK solutions, against choosing the solution nearest the previous one. (<a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/ik_branch_selection.mp4'>MP4</a>)</figcaption></figure><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='workspace' data-view='[600,1260,600,420]' data-title='Workspace'></span><h2>Where the hand can go</h2><p>Which targets have any answer at all? The reachable positions are g(Q<sub>valid</sub>). For our ideal arm with no joint limits, that is the ring on the board, from 0.2 to 1.8 around the shoulder. Joint limits, self-collision, link thickness, obstacles and the table all carve away more.</p><p>For the Panda, I <a href='https://github.com/Hadrien-Cornier/maniskill-playground/tree/main/experiments/workspace'>drew 300,000 configurations</a> uniformly inside the seven arm-joint limits, fingers open, and 263,270 passed the recorded self- and environment-collision checks (table, ground and a task cube). To write orientation down, take the gripper's three axes in world coordinates and stack them as the columns of a matrix R. The axes stay perpendicular and have length 1, which is RᵀR = I, and they describe a rotation with no mirror flip, which is det R = 1. These rotation matrices make up SO(3), and position plus orientation, (p, R), make a <strong>pose</strong> in SE(3).</p><figure><video controls muted playsinline preload='metadata' poster='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_wine_orientation.png' style='max-width:100%'><source src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_wine_orientation.mp4' type='video/mp4'><a href='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/franka_wine_orientation.mp4'>Open the video</a></video><figcaption>Simulated Panda: the target point stays fixed while the hand orientation changes. For the hand's own axis, the extra rotation goes on the right: R<sub>new</sub> = R<sub>old</sub> R<sub>z</sub>(30°).</p><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/04_rotation_columns.png' alt='Rotated basis vectors drawn next to the columns of the rotation matrix' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>Rotate the basis vectors, and they become the columns of R.</figcaption></figure><span class='rc-mark' data-panel='commands' data-view='[600,1680,600,420]' data-title='Commands'></span><h2>Who writes the target?</h2><ul><li>A classical pipeline picks a grasp pose, solves IK for the joint endpoint, plans a collision-free path and its timing, and sends the intermediate joint targets to position-controlled servos.</li><li><a href='https://tonyzhaozh.github.io/aloha/'>ACT</a>, in its original ALOHA setup, reads images and measured joint positions and predicts a chunk of future joint targets. Consecutive chunks can disagree about the same future instant, so we can average the overlapping predictions to smooth the commands; this optional step is called temporal ensembling.</li><li>A learned model predicts hand poses or displacements in task space, and IK or a Cartesian controller converts them into commands the robot accepts.</li><li>A <a href='https://palm-e.github.io/'>VLM</a> (vision-language model) can pick a high-level goal while a <a href='https://arxiv.org/html/2406.09246v3#S3'>VLA</a> (vision-language-action model) produces the motion. Some VLAs read images and instructions directly, with outputs in joint or task space.</li></ul><figure><img src='/assets/robotics/arm-control/v3/command_questions.png' alt='Planned and learned command sources converging on servo feedback' loading='lazy' style='max-width:100%;height:auto'><figcaption>Planned and learned command sources, all converging on servo feedback.</figcaption></figure><p>Whichever you pick, the command has to match what the controller accepts: position, velocity or torque, in the right units, coordinate frame and timing.</p><p>When an arm misbehaves, you now know where to look: an unreachable pose, an IK branch flip, gains tuned for another load, or a command in the wrong frame.</p><p class='rc-notes'><small>Robot renderings come from simulation; the planar and control animations are teaching schematics.
