A **value** predicts future reward. A state value tells you what to expect from a situation, while an **action value**, written $Q(s,a)$, tells you what to expect after action $a$ in state $s$. A **policy** chooses actions.

## A value-based family tree, one problem at a time

Each numbered branch names something the parent method can't do well, then points to a method that changes that mechanism. Monte Carlo, for example, waits until the episode ends to use its full return, and Q-learning replaces that unfinished tail with a guess. Learning from the gap between one guess and the next is **temporal-difference (TD)** learning. A blue node names the part of the series that works through it, and the map comes from my game [Breaking Bellman](https://hadrien-cornier.github.io/breaking-bellman/).

Tap a branch number for its problem, or a method for a card with its dates and a checked quote. On a phone, the tree turns into a list that you scroll inside the drawing.

Is this all of classical RL? No. Most of these nine are the table methods in the first part of [Sutton and Barto's textbook](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf), and DQN carries Q-learning over to a neural network. The tree leaves out actor-critic methods, policy gradients such as PPO, SAC, model-based RL, offline RL and RLHF. You'd run the table versions as they are only on problems small enough for a table, like this grid. DQN runs whole, with later add-ons such as [Double DQN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461). In bigger methods they live on as parts: PPO estimates advantages from TD errors, and TD3 and SAC keep replay, target networks and a second Q estimate.

<details class="ft-detail" id="ft-methods"><summary>The nine methods: dates, context and a checked quote</summary><p>Tap a method in the tree to open its card, or read them all here. Each card gives two dates where they differ: when the idea was first published, and when it was shown to work, proved or named. Every quote was checked against the original text on 22 October 2026.</p><section class="ft-method" data-node="trial" id="ft-m-trial"><h4>Trial and error</h4><p class="ft-m-when">Puzzle-box experiments published 1898 &middot; classic statement of the Law of Effect 1911 (Edward Thorndike). The idea itself is older.</p><p>Keep what was followed by a good result and drop what was followed by a bad one. Thorndike named this the Law of Effect after watching cats learn to escape puzzle boxes.</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">What it fixes.</span> Nobody has to show the right answer. The result itself is the teacher.</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">Where it still fails.</span> It doesn&rsquo;t say when to try something new instead of repeating what worked (branch 1), or which earlier action earned a result that arrives late (branch 2).</p><blockquote>&ldquo;Of several responses made to the same situation, those which are accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction to the animal will, other things being equal, be more firmly connected with the situation, so that, when it recurs, they will be more likely to recur&nbsp;&hellip;&rdquo;</blockquote><p class="ft-m-cite">Edward L. Thorndike, <i>Animal Intelligence: Experimental Studies</i> (Macmillan, 1911), p. 244. <a href="https://archive.org/details/animalintelligen00thor">Source</a></p></section><section class="ft-method" data-node="explore" id="ft-m-explore"><h4>&epsilon;-greedy exploration</h4><p class="ft-m-when">Idea in Watkins&rsquo;s 1989 thesis, and earlier use is likely &middot; the name in print by 1996 (Richard Sutton)</p><p>Usually take the action with the best current estimate, but a small fraction &epsilon; of the time pick one at random. Every action keeps getting tried, so a lucky early estimate can still be corrected.</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">What it fixes.</span> A greedy learner that locks onto whatever looked good first (branch 1).</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">Where it still fails.</span> With a fixed &epsilon;, it explores at the same rate forever, and its random tries ignore what the learner already knows.</p><blockquote>&ldquo;In our experiments, actions were selected according to what we call the ε-greedy policy.&rdquo;</blockquote><p class="ft-m-cite">Richard S. There is no model and no guessing: every target is a real outcome.</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">What it fixes.</span> Credit for a reward that arrives late (branch 2). The whole return reaches every earlier choice.</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">Where it still fails.</span> Nothing updates until the episode ends, which is slow for long episodes and impossible when an episode never ends (branch 3).</p><blockquote>&ldquo;Only on the completion of an episode are value estimates and policies changed.&rdquo;</blockquote><p class="ft-m-cite">Richard S. It learns the value of the greedy policy while the agent explores, which makes it off-policy. </p><p><span class="ft-m-label">What it fixes.</span> Waiting for the episode to end (branch 3).</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">Where it still fails.</span> One-step backups move a late reward slowly (4). The values ignore the agent&rsquo;s own exploratory slips, so it walks the cliff edge (5). The max over noisy estimates runs high (7), and a table needs a row for every state (8).</p><blockquote>&ldquo;Although this is a very simple idea, it has not, as far as I know, been suggested previously.&rdquo;</blockquote><p>In his next sentence, Watkins added that someone had probably considered it before.</p><p class="ft-m-cite">Christopher J. C. H. Watkins, <i>Learning from Delayed Rewards</i>, PhD thesis, University of Cambridge (1989), p. 96. <a href="http://www.cs.rhul.ac.uk/~chrisw/new_thesis.pdf">Source</a></p></section><section class="ft-method" data-node="nstep" id="ft-m-nstep"><h4>n-step TD (temporal difference)</h4><p class="ft-m-when">Proposed 1989 (Watkins, PhD thesis), a year after TD(&lambda;) (Sutton, 1988) &middot; shown practical by Cichosz (1995) and van Seijen (2016)</p><p>TD stands for temporal difference: the update learns from the gap between a prediction now and a later one. n-step TD uses n real rewards, then one guess for the rest. With n = 1 you get the one-step update. With n long enough to reach the end, you get Monte Carlo.</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">What it fixes.</span> One-step backups that pass a late reward back one state per update (branch 4).</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">Where it still fails.</span> Each update waits n steps, and when those rewards come from a different policy (replayed or off-policy data) they need corrections.</p><p><span class="ft-m-label">TD and dopamine.</span> Wolfram Schultz&rsquo;s lab recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys in the 1980s and early 1990s: they fire more when a reward is better than predicted, hold steady when it arrives as predicted, and fire less when it is worse. The TD part shows in two details: after training, the burst moves from the reward to the cue that predicts it, and a missing reward causes a dip at the time the reward was due. It&rsquo;s a strong model of one dopamine signal, not a copy of the brain. Dopamine neurons also respond to novel stimuli, their role in punishment is still debated, mouse recordings suggest they encode a spread of possible rewards, and a 2022 study argues that dopamine release in one pathway signals learned causes of reward, not prediction errors.</p><blockquote>&ldquo;n-step methods span a spectrum with MC methods at one end and one-step TD methods at the other. ## Q-learning on a grid

Q-learning keeps one score for every cell and move: an estimate of all the reward still to come after that move, with later rewards counting a little less. It nudges one score after every step ([Sutton and Barto, §6.5](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf)). The drawing lays that table out on the grid itself, so where a number sits tells you its cell and its move.

The grid has 4 rows and 12 columns. You start at the bottom left, the flag sits at the bottom right, and the ten cells between them are cliff. Every move costs −1, a step into the cliff costs −100 and sends you back to the start, and a move into the outer wall leaves you in place. Every score starts at 0, so the grid in the drawing is empty for now.

## The table

Each cell is cut along its diagonals into four triangles, one per move: the top triangle holds the score for moving up, the right one for moving right, and so on. That score is $Q(s,a)$, for move $a$ in cell $s$, and less negative is better. Red triangles are moves into the cliff, and the arrow in each cell points at its highest score.

The drawing now shows the recorded table after 10,000 updates. Follow the arrows from the start: up once, right eleven times along the edge of the cliff, then down onto the flag. That is the shortest route, 13 moves. Every triangle that points down into the cliff from that row is deep red, about −112, because that move falls.

The bar above the drawing replays the run. Step one update at a time or drag the slider: the yellow triangle is the score the update changes, and the red square follows its cell. ## Two readouts of one table

The robot needs both a prediction and a choice. In the start cell, up scores −12.25 and right scores −112.13. The close-up under the map reads both from the same four triangles: the value is the largest score, −12.25, and the move is the one that has it, up.

$$\begin{aligned} V_Q(s) &= \max_a Q(s,a) \\ \pi_Q(s) &= \arg\max_a Q(s,a) \end{aligned}$$

<details>
<summary>Notation: Q, V, π and the star</summary>

Read $Q(s,a)$ as the learned score for action $a$ in state $s$. From those four scores, $V_Q(s)$ takes the largest and the policy $\pi_Q(s)$ takes the corresponding action. So $\max$ returns a number, while $\arg\max$ returns an action, using a fixed rule for ties. A star marks the optimal quantities: $q_*$ is the optimal action value, $v_*$ the optimal state value and $\pi_*$ an optimal action choice. A subscript $Q$ means a readout of the current learned table, which need not be optimal. With $Q=q_*$, these readouts are optimal.

## The update

The Bellman equation splits the future into one reward and the next state's value, and Q-learning turns that into an update. The target is the reward plus the discounted best score in the next cell, and the old score moves part of the way toward it:

<div class="eq-label">Q-learning update <span>Watkins 1989; Sutton and Barto, Eq. 6.8</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + \alpha\,\Big[\,&\underbrace{r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s',a')}_{\text{target}} \\ &- Q(s,a)\Big] \end{aligned}$$

The target uses one observed move in place of the Bellman equation's average, and the current $Q$ stands in for the future. Learning from that estimate is **bootstrapping**. The bracket, target minus old score, is the **TD error**: how far one step's outcome missed the table's prediction.

Here the drawing jumps to update 6 and works it by hand. In the start cell the robot tries left, bumps the wall and stays put, for a reward of −1. The yellow triangle is the score that changes, and the blue one is the best score it borrows from where it landed, the start cell again, at −0.5. So the target is $-1+0.99(-0.5)=-1.495$ and the TD error is $-1.495-(-0.5)=-0.995$, worse than predicted. With a step size of 0.5 the old −0.5 moves halfway, to −0.9975. The −50 in that cell came from update 5, a step off the cliff. Step the bar and the board redraws for any of the first 400 updates.

Dopamine neurons in monkeys fire more when a reward beats the prediction and less when it falls short, a lot like a TD error ([Schultz, Dayan and Montague, 1997](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/sdm97.pdf)). The n-step TD card in the tree says where that match stops.

## Credit travels backward

How does a cost at one cell reach a choice made before arrival? Look at two neighbors in the drawing: the cell just above the start, and the cell one step to its right.

At update 19 the robot moves right from the cell above the start, and that score falls from 0 to −0.5. It was the cell's last zero, so its best score drops to −0.5 too. $$\begin{aligned}
\text{target}_{24} &= -1 + 0.99(-0.5) \\
&= -1.495 \\
Q_{\text{new}}(\text{neighbor},\text{left}) &= 0 + 0.5(-1.495 - 0) \\
&= -0.7475
\end{aligned}$$

With one-step targets, each round moves the reward back by one state, because a state can only read the next one, and the next one only changed last round. S15 gets its value in round 1 and S0 in round 16. With eight-step targets, round 1 already fills in S8 to S15, since their eight steps reach the end without reading the table. A wrong estimate enters the one-step target almost whole: $0.95\times0.4=0.38$. The eight-step target reads the table only after eight real rewards, so the same error is scaled down to $0.95^8\times0.4\approx0.27$. That's the **bias** from an inaccurate continuation, and a longer target has less of it. In exchange, the eight-step target contains whatever actually happened in those eight steps. The one-off −2 moves it by $0.95^3\times2\approx1.71$ in this attempt, and the one-step target doesn't see it at all, because the estimate it reads is an average over many attempts.

That doesn't mean a bad later step erases the credit for a good early one. Averaged over many attempts, later outcomes are part of the right answer for the policy that produced them. What they add is **variance**: each single target swings more. So a longer target trades error from the estimate for noise from later outcomes, and which one matters more depends on how good the estimates are and how random the later steps are. [Part 2's horizon section](/robotics/rl-2-bootstrapping/#sec-horizon) explains why that isn't a fixed rule. One more catch for Q-learning: it wants the value of the greedy policy, so if the later steps were exploratory moves, an eight-step target measures the exploring policy instead. Off-policy n-step methods correct for that ([Sutton and Barto, Section 7.3](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf)).

## When it converges

A route can work while other scores are still wrong. The guarantee covers the whole table in the limit, if every cell and move keeps getting tried and the step size shrinks without stopping learning. The drawing shows why: one sweep of expected updates multiplies the largest gap between two guesses of the table by at most $\gamma = 0.99$. The run's fixed step size of 0.5 is outside the guarantee.

<details>
<summary>The exact conditions, and why the expected target is stable</summary>

Let $n$ count all updates, with $Q_n$ the current table and $q_*$ the optimal table. Each state-action pair has its own update count $k$ and step sizes $\alpha_k$. The first sum below requires unlimited total adjustment, and the second limits the accumulated squared step size. For example, $\alpha_k=1/(k+1)$ for $k=1,2,\dots$ satisfies both. The arrow $\to$ means convergence:

$$\begin{aligned} &\sum_k \alpha_k = \infty, \quad \sum_k \alpha_k^2 < \infty \\ &\Longrightarrow\; Q_n(s,a) \to q_*(s,a) \ \text{with probability 1} \end{aligned}$$

Why is the expected target stable? Let $T_*$ map a whole candidate table to its expected Bellman target. Start with two candidate tables $q,u$ and call their largest absolute entrywise difference $e$. Taking a maximum and averaging cannot increase that difference. Discounting then bounds the new difference by $\gamma e$, and the drawing applies this bound twice with $\gamma=0.99$.


This guarantee assumes:

- **Finite, stationary task.** Finitely many states and actions; fixed Markov reward and transition laws.
- **Bounded rewards.** Finite reward magnitudes and discount $0 \le \gamma < 1$.
- **Repeated updates.** Every nonterminal pair is sampled infinitely often, with step sizes in $[0,1)$ satisfying both sums.

[Watkins and Dayan (1992)](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/cjch.pdf), pages 280 to 282, prove this discounted case. The recorded run uses fixed $\alpha=0.5$, outside the guarantee.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Pros and cons of a Q table</summary>

A small table lets you inspect every prediction, as we did here. Each weakness below points to the part that addresses it; the run counts come from the data drawer and are not a general ranking.

**Pros**

- **No model.** Learn from moves and results.
- **Immediate updates.** Learn every step, while behavior can include exploration.
- **Readable estimates.** Print the table and inspect what each choice expects.

**Cons and next methods**

- **Exploration costs.** 171 falls in 10,000 updates; 150 came after update 2,000, all on nonfavorite moves. [On-policy targets](/robotics/rl-4-on-policy-and-off-policy/) address this.
- **Table growth.** 37 decision states require 148 action values. [Function approximation](/robotics/rl-3-function-approximation/) shares estimates across situations.
- **Noisy maxima.** Lucky estimates favor worse choices. Double Q separates picking from scoring, costing a second table. Open “Noisy maxima” below.
- **Approximation stability.** Bootstrapping with function approximation and off-policy data can diverge: [the deadly triad in part 5](/robotics/rl-5-the-deadly-triad/).

Averaging many noisy maxima tends to run high. Let $i$ index machines and $X_i$ be machine $i$'s noisy estimate. $\mathbb E$ averages over that estimation noise, $\max_i$ takes the largest machine estimate and $\ge$ means at least. $$\mathbb{E}\big[\max_i X_i\big] \;\ge\; \max_i \mathbb{E}[X_i]$$

For unbiased machine estimates, the right side is −0.1; selecting their maximum can raise the left side. This is maximization bias (Sutton and Barto, Example 6.7).

<details id="sec-bandit">
<summary>A one-state example: exploration at the slot machines</summary>

The grid adds consequences that unfold over time. A bandit isolates the exploration problem: every pull returns to the same situation. Its one-row table estimates the immediate payout, so we can see the effect of trying other actions without a later-state target.

### Explore or exploit: the bandit

Which of ten slot machines should you pull? Each pays a hidden average plus noise. This **bandit** has one state, so its table has one row: $Q(a)$ estimates machine $a$'s mean payout; $n(a)$ counts its pulls, including the latest one, and $r$ is that pull's reward. Let $\varepsilon$ be the probability of exploring with a uniformly random machine.

$$Q(a) \leftarrow Q(a) + \tfrac{1}{n(a)}\bigl(r - Q(a)\bigr)$$

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Ten slot machines, 1,000 pulls (ε = 0 is greedy, ε = 0.1 explores a little)</div>
<ol>
<li>Set Q(a) = 0 and n(a) = 0 for each machine a.</li>
<li>Repeat for 1,000 pulls:</li>
<li class="i1">With probability ε pick a random machine. Otherwise pick argmax Q(a), at random on a tie.</li>
<li class="i1">Pull it and observe the payout r. Set n(a) = n(a) + 1.</li>
<li class="i1">Q(a) ← Q(a) + (r − Q(a)) / n(a)</li>
</ol>
</div>

The factor $1/n(a)$ makes this a running sample average: each new payout receives one equal share of the updated mean.

For example, a machine with two payouts totaling 4 has estimate 2. A third payout of −1 gives $2+(-1-2)/3=1$, the mean of all three payouts. The table stores that running mean, not the latest reward. [Sutton and Barto, §2.4](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf). Greedy picks the largest estimate and can stay with a lucky early choice. With $\varepsilon=0.1$, one pull in ten explores, so other estimates get a chance to improve.

<details id="sec-explore-more">
<summary>Two other ways to explore: start optimistic, or add a bonus</summary>

The slot-machine drawer above sets up the simplest case: ten machines, each paying a hidden average plus noise, and a one-row table where $Q(a)$ is the running average of machine $a$'s payouts. Picking a random machine one pull in ten, as ε-greedy does, put 77.5% of the last 200 pulls on the best machine, against 35.5% for pure greedy. But random pulls never stop. Even once you know the best machine, one pull in ten goes somewhere else. Two other rules aim the exploration at what you haven't tried.

**Start optimistic.** Set every estimate to +5, far above any machine's likely average, and act greedily. Each pull disappoints, so the pulled machine's estimate drops and the learner moves to another one still at +5, until every machine has been tried. [Sutton and Barto, §2.6](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf), show this with a constant step size of 0.1. With a running average, the first payout replaces the start value completely, since $1/n(a)=1$ on the first pull. So optimism buys exactly one pull of each machine. The book also calls it far from a generally useful approach: the push to explore is temporary, so it can't help when the machines change over time.

**Add a bonus for what you haven't tried.** The **upper confidence bound (UCB)** rule picks

$$A_t=\arg\max_a\Big[Q_t(a)+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N_t(a)}}\,\Big],$$

where $t$ counts all pulls so far, $N_t(a)$ is the same count as $n(a)$, and $c>0$ sets the size of the bonus ([§2.7, Eq. 2.10](http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf)). A machine that has never been pulled counts as the largest, so each one gets tried first. The bonus shrinks as a machine gets pulled and grows slowly while it's left alone. The book's limits: UCB is harder than random pulls to extend beyond bandits, to machines that change, and to large state spaces, and it's usually not practical with function approximation.

| Greedy, start 0, running average | 35.5% |
| Greedy, start +5, running average | 63.5% |
| Greedy, start 0, step 0.1 | 51.4% |
| Greedy, start +5, step 0.1 | 83.5% |
| UCB, $c=2$, running average | 82.0% |

The log's explanation, backed by a follow-up probe: a constant step keeps only about the last 20 payouts, so its error stops shrinking, while the bonus keeps shrinking as if the estimate were still getting more precise. And the constant step alone lifts plain greedy from 35.5% to 51.4%, a gain the log doesn't explain.

<div class="eq-label">A state's value is an average of its actions' values</div>

$$v_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\, q_\pi(s,a)$$

<div class="eq-label">For the best values, take the max in place of the average</div>

$$\begin{aligned} v_*(s) &= \max_a q_*(s,a) \\ \pi_*(s) &= \arg\max_a q_*(s,a) \end{aligned}$$

Average over the actions the policy picks and you get the value of a state. Take the best action in place of the average and you get the best value.

</details>

### Where the Bellman equation comes from

How can you learn about a whole future from one transition? Here $G_t$ is the return from step $t$, $R_{t+1}$ is its first reward and $G_{t+1}$ is the remaining return. Split after that first reward, then take the expectation. The subscript $\pi$ on an expectation means following policy $\pi$; a vertical bar means conditioning on the stated starting state or action. The Bellman equation links each state's value to the next state's value.

$$G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1}$$

Factor out $\gamma$, then average over possible futures.

<div class="eq-label">Bellman equation for a policy <span>Eq. 3.14</span></div>

$$v_\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[R_{t+1} + \gamma\, v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s\right]$$

The first equation follows policy $\pi$. For optimal action values, fix the current action $a$, average over the world's resulting transition, then choose the best action after arrival:

<div class="eq-label">Bellman optimality: maximize over the next action <span>Eq. 3.20</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} q_*(s,a) = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[&R_{t+1} + \gamma \max_{a'} q_*(S_{t+1}, a') \\ &\Bigm| S_t = s,\, A_t = a\Bigr] \end{aligned}$$

Once you have $q_*$, taking the action with the largest value in each state is an optimal policy, so solving the second equation solves the problem.

<details>
<summary>Derivation from the return</summary>

The steps below expand, split and average the same reward sequence. $k$ counts rewards ahead of the current time. A fixed policy makes the conditional expected remaining return equal to its value at the next state. This is the bridge from recorded rewards to a reusable prediction.

<div class="eq-label">1. The return <span>Eq. 3.8</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \cdots \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} \end{aligned}$$

<div class="eq-label">2. Factor out γ <span>Eq. 3.9</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} G_t &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\bigl(R_{t+2} + \gamma R_{t+3} + \cdots\bigr) \\ &= R_{t+1} + \gamma\, G_{t+1} \end{aligned}$$

<div class="eq-label">3. The episode ends at step T: nothing is left</div>

$$G_T = 0$$

So the value of a terminal state is 0.

<div class="eq-label">4. Values are expected returns <span>Eq. 3.12 and 3.13</span></div>

$$\begin{aligned} v_\pi(s) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s\right] \\ q_\pi(s,a) &= \mathbb{E}_\pi\!\left[G_t \mid S_t = s,\, A_t = a\right] \end{aligned}$$

Putting equation 2 into equation 4 gives the Bellman equation for a policy. The value is not a new reward: $R_{t+1}$ is real, and $\gamma\, v_\pi(S_{t+1})$ stands in for everything after it.

</details>

<summary>Double Q-learning: separate picking from scoring</summary>

### Pick and score separately

When estimates are noisy, picking the largest also tends to pick lucky noise. **[Double Q-learning](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2010/hash/091d584fced301b442654dd8c23b3fc9-Abstract.html)** reduces this bias: one table picks the next action and the other scores it. Write those tables as $Q_1$ and $Q_2$; $\text{target}_1$ is the target when updating the first.

$$\text{target}_1 = r + \gamma\, Q_2\big(s', \arg\max_{a'} Q_1(s',a')\big)$$

**Use:** reduce maximization bias. **Needs:** separately learned estimates. **Cost:** two tables sharing the updates. The [seventh branch of the tree](#ft-edge-7) shows a noisy selection and a separate score.

<details>
<summary>Algorithm: Double Q-learning</summary>

Keep both tables across steps. When updating $Q_1$, select the action with $Q_1$ and evaluate it with $Q_2$; swap the roles when updating $Q_2$. Apply the usual step-size update to the chosen table only. Separate scoring reduces selection bias; it does not guarantee an unbiased estimate at every finite step. [Van Hasselt, 2010](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2010/hash/091d584fced301b442654dd8c23b3fc9-Abstract.html).

<div class="algo">
<div class="algo-title">Double Q-learning target</div>
<ol>
<li>Choose a table to update at random: each receives half the updates on average.</li>
<li>That table picks the best next action. The other table scores it.</li>
<li>Build the target from r plus γ times that score; update only the chosen table. Terminal target = r.</li>
</ol>

The source's $x$ and $a$ mean state and action. Watkins and Dayan is [*Q-learning*](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~dayan/papers/cjch.pdf), Machine Learning 8, pages 279 to 292, which I read as page images.

- **Notation.** The book writes the reward after step $t$ as $R_{t+1}$. The update is Eq. 6.8, and $q_*$ is defined in Section 3.6 with Eq. 3.16; Eq. 3.17 and Eq. 3.20 write it in terms of $v_*$ and as a Bellman equation.
- **Greedy readouts.** Watkins and Dayan, pages 280 to 281: $V^*(x) = \max_a Q^*(x,a)$, and the policy that takes a maximizing action is optimal. Page 281 says early Q values may not reflect the policy they define.
- **Convergence.** Pages 280 to 282 set up a finite Markov task with fixed transition and reward laws, discount below 1, table representation, bounded rewards, repeated sampling and the per-pair step-size conditions. Section 4 treats the undiscounted absorbing extension separately. I did not check the convergence outline in Appendix 1 of the 1989 thesis.
- **Maximization bias.** Sections 6.7 and Example 6.7. In the book the left action leads to many choices with mean −0.1. The game uses ten.
