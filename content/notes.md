## Hiring

Hiring engineers is an attempt to find the right combination of talent, motivation, and commitment. I think of talent as shaping the rate at which someone can learn and create more value. Motivation determines how much of that ability they apply. Commitment gives that growth time to compound.

By commitment, I do not mean loyalty for its own sake. People do their best work when they have enough time to understand the company, build judgment, and learn from the consequences of their decisions. The company has a responsibility to create an environment worth staying in and to keep earning that commitment.

The difficulty is that an interview is a short sample of what may become a long working relationship. In that limited time, I am especially tuned to passion, curiosity, and an engineering mindset. I want to understand what draws someone into a problem, how quickly they learn, and whether they care enough to keep looking after the obvious answer.

I like to start with something a candidate knows well and ask progressively deeper questions. A few follow-ups often reveal how thoroughly they understand their work and what interests them enough to keep digging. I am skeptical of LeetCode-style questions as a way to identify good engineers. I prefer logic problems that let me see how someone structures their thinking. Can they follow an argument through to its conclusion? What happens when I offer a counterexample?

Those abilities matter even more with AI tools. AI can produce a plausible answer quickly, but the engineer still has to examine the reasoning, find weak assumptions, and test counterexamples. I look for people who can use the tools without giving up the judgment that makes the result trustworthy.

## Product strategy

I often find myself disagreeing with product directions that sound like common sense, especially when the argument begins with what other companies in the industry offer. A product can create revenue or switching costs and still be strategically weak. I care more about whether it adds real value in a way that can strengthen over time.

I look for products we can keep building on. Each investment should improve our ability to solve the underlying customer problem and make the next investment more valuable. Stickiness by itself is not much of an advantage if the product is easy to copy or does not make us better at solving the customer's core problem.

In recruiting advertising, the raw material is scarce job-seeker attention and the outcome is hires. We sit between those two points, so I ask whether a product makes us better at turning one into the other. That is where we add value, and where continued investment can create a durable advantage.

This is why I am skeptical of becoming an applicant tracking system simply because it would be a large, sticky product. Being the system of record is a different role from making recruiting attention more effective. I view a resume database the same way. Selling access may monetize an asset, but using those signals to improve hiring outcomes strengthens the capability at the center of the business.

## The poison of switching costs

Switching costs are a powerful moat, but they can also make a company worse over time. When customers keep paying because leaving is difficult, revenue becomes less connected to product quality. The company loses some of the pressure that keeps it sharp.

At Talroo, we avoid long-term customer contracts. There is a real tradeoff here. Predictable cash flow contributes to a company's value, and short commitments make that predictability harder. But they also force us to keep earning our customers' spend through the quality of our product and service.

I think the same principle applies to the software we buy. A large annual commitment, especially one that exceeds our actual usage, weakens the incentive to optimize. Once the money is committed, waste becomes easier to tolerate.

I want us to preserve the pressure to improve. Engineers should always have a reason to reduce costs, raise standards, and adapt. Some uncertainty is healthy when it keeps us honest.

## Advertising

I think of advertising as allocating scarce human attention. Advertisers face a tradeoff between urgency and efficiency, and we have a finite number of impressions from which to generate meaningful conversions.

Improving engagement and application rates can make that attention more productive. But removing every step can also make it easier to submit applications with very little intent. A higher conversion count can hide a worse outcome for the employer.

Some friction is useful when it helps establish that a person has considered the opportunity. I care about whether an application represents real interest and whether the employer gets something valuable from it. I think optimizing for low-intent volume pushes the industry toward commoditization and a race to the bottom.

## Communication is part of the work

I think of a company as an organism. The CEO, or whoever is coordinating a decision, needs information to move through it quickly. Upward communication matters because a blocker in one part of the company may be easy to solve somewhere else.

Good updates also create the peace of mind that makes delegation possible. When someone communicates progress without being asked, the manager can focus elsewhere with confidence that the work is moving. A useful update answers the status question before it needs to be asked.

I tell engineers that promotion requires creating value and communicating value. The work has to matter, and other people need enough visibility to understand its effect. Doing useful work matters, but career growth also depends on making the work and its impact visible.

I learned to treat communication as part of the work itself. I try to practice that consistently and encourage it across the company.

## Principles for engineers

When I asked myself how to become a better manager, I wrote down the behaviors that had helped me earn trust and get promoted. These are the same principles I ask engineers on my teams to develop. Together, they describe how I think engineers create value, work well with others, and become people the company can rely on.

1. **Start with business impact.** Technical work matters when it improves the company or the customer experience. Understand the outcome you are trying to create before choosing what to build.
2. **Stay curious and keep learning.** Seek out better tools and ways of working. Test them, form your own judgment, and share what you learn. This matters especially with AI, where the tools often move faster than established practices.
3. **Collaborate early.** Bring other people in before uncertainty becomes rework. Ask for input, share unfinished thinking, and solve difficult problems together.
4. **Plan before you build.** Clarify the problem and sketch the system before writing code. A short conversation, a whiteboard session, or a clear diagram can prevent days of unnecessary work.
5. **Practice craftsmanship.** Treat working software as the starting point. Pay attention to reliability, clarity, usability, maintainability, and the details that determine whether the result is truly complete.
6. **Take ownership.** Finish what you start. Follow the work through to its real outcome, and surface blockers early enough for other people to help.
7. **Communicate proactively.** Listen carefully, ask clarifying questions, and confirm shared understanding. Keep people informed about progress, decisions, risks, and blockers before they need to ask. Career growth depends on creating value and making that value visible.
8. **Think in systems.** Look beyond the task in front of you. Ask whether the underlying process could be simpler, faster, more reliable, or automated. Fix patterns, not just individual symptoms.

## Learning and doing

Jim Keller influenced how I think about engineering, especially the balance between learning and doing. My instinct has usually been to slow down and understand a problem thoroughly. Years of engineering have taught me to ask whether more understanding will change the decision in front of me.

Sometimes it will, and taking the time is worthwhile. Other times, the better choice is to act on what we know and move to the next priority. I think I have a better balance now. I still value understanding, but I’m more deliberate about where it helps and when it’s time to get something done.
